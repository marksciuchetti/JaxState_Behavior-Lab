# Model paragraphs for the analysis, results, and discussion write up (Workshop 8)

**SIMULATED DATA.** The paragraphs below are models for students. Every statistic in them comes from the SIMULATED Multimodal Ad Study of Workshop 7 (seed 20271231) and was copied from `analysis_output.txt`, the full console log of `analysis_full.R`; numbers are rounded to two decimals (p values to three). No real person was recorded, ALTAVO and BRENLOW are invented brands, and the numbers must never be quoted as findings. The paragraphs show the form of a results and discussion section for a marketing journal: how to state the analysis strategy, how to separate planned from exploratory analyses, how to report every test with an effect size and a confidence interval, and how to discuss what multimodal data can and cannot show.

**Continuity with Workshop 7.** H1 to H3 were run again with the same data and the same code; STEP 4 of `analysis_full.R` confirmed that all 55 printed lines and all 94 stored values match Workshop 7 exactly. The H1 to H3 paragraphs below are therefore copied word for word from Workshop 7 (`ws7/data/results_text.md`). Data preparation (exports, keys, exclusion rules R1 to R3, the merge) is reported in Workshop 7, section (a), and is not repeated here.

## (a) Analysis strategy

**Measures.** The analysis used one row per participant and stimulus from the Workshop 7 data set (22 participants after exclusion rule R2; 21 for analyses of GSR during Ad_B after rule R3). Visual attention was the gaze based dwell time on three areas of interest (AOIs) on each ad, expressed as a percentage of the 30 s ad (Smart Eye Aurora, 60 Hz; fixations classified by the study default I-VT filter); a participant counted as having fixated the Logo when the AOI export recorded at least one fixation in it. Facial expression was the percentage of time that the Affectiva AFFDEX classifier scored Joy, Engagement, or Brow Furrow at or above a threshold of 50. Arousal was the number of skin conductance (GSR) peaks per minute of valid signal from the iMotions GSR peak detection notebook, during the 60 s Baseline and during each ad. Self report comprised liking of each ad (1 to 7), brand recall for each ad (correct or not), a three item attitude scale toward reusable water bottles, and a single purchase intention item that asked about reusable water bottles in general, once, after both ads.

**Planned and exploratory analyses.** All tests were two tailed with alpha = .05, and every planned test is reported regardless of its outcome. Three hypotheses were written in the study protocol before data collection (H1, Logo fixation and brand recall; H2, Joy and liking; H3, GSR peaks per minute during each ad versus Baseline) and were tested in Workshop 7. Four further questions were specified in a written analysis plan (`SIMULATED_analysis_plan.csv`) after data collection but before these analyses were run: attention by ad and AOI (Q4), facial expression by ad (Q5), the head to head comparisons that the client requested on liking, recall, and arousal (Q6), and a check of the counterbalancing (O1). Because Q4 to Q6 were fixed after the data existed, they carry less confirmatory weight than H1 to H3 (Nosek et al., 2018; Simmons et al., 2011). A matrix of correlations among seven participant level measures (E1) was labeled exploratory in advance; it is reported with confidence intervals and without p values, and it tests nothing.

**Multiplicity.** Each hypothesis or question defined its own family of tests. Within a family of related comparisons, p values were adjusted with Holm's (1979) sequentially rejective procedure, which keeps the probability of at least one false positive in the family at .05 and is uniformly more powerful than the Bonferroni correction: H3 (two ads), the six pairwise AOI comparisons of Q4 (Q4a), the three ad comparisons of Q4 (Q4b), the three expressions of Q5, the three client comparisons of Q6, and the eight order checks of O1. The omnibus tests of the Q4 model were not adjusted.

**Models and effect sizes.** Q4 used a linear mixed model of dwell with ad, AOI, and their interaction as fixed effects (sum to zero contrasts) and a random intercept for each participant, fitted with lme4 (Bates et al., 2015); fixed effects were tested with Type III F tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom from lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) and with likelihood ratio tests between nested models fitted by maximum likelihood. Estimated marginal means were computed from the model as linear combinations of the fixed effects. Because the three AOIs share the same 30 s, a repeated measures analysis of variance with Greenhouse and Geisser's (1959) correction served as a check that tests each effect against its own participant by effect error term. Follow up comparisons and the comparisons of Q5 and Q6 were paired t tests on each participant's own difference, reported with the mean difference and its 95% confidence interval (CI) and with Cohen's d_z (the mean difference divided by the standard deviation of the differences; Lakens, 2013), whose 95% CI was obtained from the noncentral t distribution (Cumming & Finch, 2001). Because percent of time and dwell measures pile up at zero, every paired t test was accompanied by a Wilcoxon signed rank test. Brand recall, a yes or no outcome measured twice per person, was compared with the exact McNemar test on the discordant pairs (McNemar, 1947). The order checks fitted y = ad x order + (1 | participant) for liking and for dwell on each AOI and estimated two contrasts with their CIs: the order effect (the AB group minus the BA group) and the position effect (an ad seen first minus the same ad seen second). Spearman correlations were computed at the participant level, with 95% CIs from the Fisher z transformation and the Bonett and Wright (2000) variance. A sensitivity power analysis computed the smallest effect the design could detect and the sample sizes the next study needs (Lakens, 2022); observed power was not computed because it adds nothing to the p value (Hoenig & Heisey, 2001).

**Software.** Data were collected with iMotions Lab (Version 11.1.47136.14; iMotions A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark). Analyses were run in R 4.3.3 (R Core Team, 2024) with readr 2.1.5, dplyr 1.1.4, tidyr 1.3.1, ggplot2 3.4.4 (Wickham, 2016), lme4 1.1.35.1, and lmerTest 3.1.3. The descriptive statistics, the Q5 tests, and the Holm correction were reproduced in a spreadsheet (`SIMULATED_analysis_report.xlsx`, recalculated in LibreOffice 24.2), and all 256 checked values agreed with R. Figures show every participant's values together with the means and their 95% CIs rather than bars of means (Weissgerber et al., 2015).

## (b) Results

**Descriptive statistics.** Table 1 (`SIMULATED_descriptives.csv`) reports n, mean, SD, median, interquartile range, and the percentage of zeros for every measure. Gaze dwell concentrated on the Product (Ad_A: M = 42.58 percent of the ad, SD = 8.42; Ad_B: M = 41.34, SD = 6.17), followed by the Headline (Ad_A: M = 17.84, SD = 7.12; Ad_B: M = 20.27, SD = 6.44) and the Logo (Ad_A: M = 1.78, SD = 2.82; Ad_B: M = 1.40, SD = 1.89); 10 of 22 participants never directed their gaze to the Logo in each ad (4 in both ads), and the three AOIs together held 62.61 percent of the ad on average (SD = 8.77). Facial expressions were rare at Baseline (Joy: M = 0.38 percent of time, zero for 90.91 percent of participants) and more frequent during the ads (Joy: Ad_A M = 12.13, SD = 10.81; Ad_B M = 8.33, SD = 9.46; Engagement: Ad_A M = 14.52, SD = 11.72; Ad_B M = 9.95, SD = 10.01), whereas Brow Furrow stayed near zero (Ad_A M = 0.65, SD = 1.52, zero for 81.82 percent; Ad_B M = 0.38, SD = 1.31, zero for 90.91 percent). GSR peaks per minute rose from Baseline (M = 2.06, SD = 1.86) to Ad_A (M = 7.27, SD = 6.80) and Ad_B (M = 6.00, SD = 4.94, n = 21). Mean liking was 4.59 (SD = 1.40) for Ad_A and 4.05 (SD = 1.29) for Ad_B; 5 of 22 participants (22.7%) chose 6 or 7 for Ad_A and 3 of 22 (13.6%) for Ad_B. Attitude toward reusable water bottles averaged 4.56 (SD = 1.00), and purchase intention averaged 3.68 (SD = 1.39), with 1 of 22 participants (4.5%) in the top two boxes.

**H1: Logo fixation and brand recall.** Participants fixated the Logo in 12 of 22 cases for each ad and recalled the advertised brand in 12 of 22 cases for each ad. Pooled over the two ads, the brand was recalled in 17 of 24 observations in which the Logo had been fixated (70.8%) and in 7 of 20 observations in which it had not (35.0%), OR = 4.34, 95% CI [1.08, 19.55], Fisher's exact p = .032; χ²(1, N = 44) = 5.65, p = .017, φ = .36. The association had the same direction for each ad separately, with wide intervals that included 1 (Ad_A: OR = 6.31, 95% CI [0.80, 68.43], p = .084; Ad_B: OR = 2.85, 95% CI [0.39, 24.13], p = .391). A mixed logistic regression with a random intercept per participant gave OR = 4.51, 95% CI [1.26, 16.10], z = 2.32, p = .020; the participant variance was estimated at zero (a singular fit), so the model reduced to an ordinary logistic regression. H1 was supported, with the caution that the pooled observations are not fully independent and the interval is wide.

**H2: Joy and liking.** Participants showed Joy during 12.13 percent of Ad_A (SD = 10.81) and 8.33 percent of Ad_B (SD = 9.46), with no Joy in 11 of the 44 participant by ad observations; mean liking was 4.59 (SD = 1.40) for Ad_A and 4.05 (SD = 1.29) for Ad_B. In the mixed model, liking was 0.32 points higher for every 10 percentage points of Joy, SE = 0.20, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.71], t(40.66) = 1.63, p = .110; the standardized slope was 0.24, and 37 percent of the variance in liking lay between participants (intraclass correlation = .37). The correlations for each ad were small to moderate and not significant (Ad_A: r(20) = .27, 95% CI [-.18, .62], p = .232, Spearman ρ = .33; Ad_B: r(20) = .18, 95% CI [-.26, .56], p = .424, Spearman ρ = .14). H2 was not supported at alpha = .05; the estimate was positive, but a sample of 22 participants cannot distinguish a small positive relation from none.

**H3: GSR peaks per minute.** GSR peaks were more frequent during Ad_A (M = 7.27 peaks per minute, SD = 6.80) than during Baseline (M = 2.06, SD = 1.86), mean difference = 5.21, 95% CI [2.41, 8.01], t(21) = 3.87, p < .001, d_z = 0.83, 95% CI [0.33, 1.30]; 19 of 22 participants increased. Among the 21 participants with valid GSR during Ad_B, peaks were more frequent during Ad_B (M = 6.00, SD = 4.94) than during Baseline (M = 1.88, SD = 1.68), mean difference = 4.12, 95% CI [2.15, 6.10], t(20) = 4.35, p < .001, d_z = 0.95, 95% CI [0.42, 1.46]; 16 of 21 increased. Both effects remained significant after Holm's adjustment (both adjusted p < .001). The differences for Ad_A were skewed by a few strong responders (Shapiro Wilk W = 0.862, p = .006), and the Wilcoxon signed rank tests agreed with the t tests (Ad_A: V = 212, p < .001; Ad_B: V = 165, p < .001). H3 was supported for both ads.

**Q4: Attention by ad and AOI.** Dwell differed strongly between AOIs, F(2, 126) = 508.90, p < .001, partial η² = .89 (computed from F), likelihood ratio χ²(2) = 289.06, p < .001. Neither the main effect of ad, F(1, 126) = 0.07, p = .798, χ²(1) = 0.07, p = .795, nor the ad by AOI interaction, F(2, 126) = 1.14, p = .323, partial η² = .02, χ²(2) = 2.37, p = .306, was significant: the distribution of gaze across the three AOIs did not differ detectably between the two ads (Figure 1). The participant intercept variance was estimated at zero (a singular fit, residual SD = 5.95), which is expected for shares of a fixed 30 s: a participant who dwells longer on one AOI dwells less on another, so participants differ little in their average dwell. The repeated measures check, which does not depend on that variance, led to the same conclusions (AOI: F(2, 42) = 356.54, Greenhouse Geisser ε = .76, corrected p < .001, partial η² = .94; ad by AOI: F(2, 42) = 1.37, ε = .86, corrected p = .265, partial η² = .06; ad: F(1, 21) = 0.12, p = .730). The model also assumes a common residual variance, which the AOIs do not share (residual SD 2.37 for the Logo and 7.30 for the Product; `figures/SIMULATED_assumption_checks.png`); as a consequence, the model based 95% CI for the Logo in Ad_A, [-0.73, 4.29], extends below zero, whereas the interval from the Logo values alone is [0.53, 3.03]. The paired follow up comparisons, which use each comparison's own variability, are therefore the basis for interpretation. All six pairwise AOI comparisons within the ads were significant after Holm's adjustment (all adjusted p < .001; all Wilcoxon p < .001). In Ad_A, dwell on the Product exceeded dwell on the Headline by 24.74 percentage points, 95% CI [19.03, 30.45], t(21) = 9.02, d_z = 1.92, 95% CI [1.20, 2.63], and dwell on the Logo by 40.80 points [36.71, 44.90], t(21) = 20.71, d_z = 4.42 [3.02, 5.80]; dwell on the Headline exceeded dwell on the Logo by 16.06 points [12.36, 19.76], t(21) = 9.03, d_z = 1.93 [1.20, 2.63]. In Ad_B, the corresponding differences were 21.08 points [17.31, 24.85], t(21) = 11.62, d_z = 2.48 [1.62, 3.32]; 39.95 points [37.03, 42.87], t(21) = 28.44, d_z = 6.06 [4.19, 7.93]; and 18.87 points [15.91, 21.83], t(21) = 13.28, d_z = 2.83 [1.88, 3.77]. No AOI held more gaze in one ad than in the other (Q4b; Ad_A minus Ad_B): Logo, 0.39 points, 95% CI [-1.23, 2.00], t(21) = 0.50, p = .625, Holm p = 1.000, d_z = 0.11 [-0.31, 0.52]; Product, 1.24 points [-2.76, 5.24], t(21) = 0.64, p = .526, Holm p = 1.000, d_z = 0.14 [-0.28, 0.56]; Headline, -2.42 points [-5.90, 1.05], t(21) = -1.45, p = .162, Holm p = .485, d_z = -0.31 [-0.73, 0.12]; the Wilcoxon tests agreed (Logo: V = 85.5, p = 1.000; Headline: V = 85, p = .183).

**Q5: Facial expression by ad.** None of the three expressions differed significantly between the ads (Figure 2). Joy was more frequent during Ad_A than during Ad_B by 3.80 percentage points of time, 95% CI [-1.55, 9.16], t(21) = 1.48, p = .155, Holm p = .331, d_z = 0.31, 95% CI [-0.12, 0.74]; 12 participants showed more Joy during Ad_A, 7 during Ad_B, and 3 showed none in either ad (Wilcoxon V = 128.5, p = .184). Engagement was more frequent during Ad_A by 4.57 points [-1.13, 10.26], t(21) = 1.67, p = .110, Holm p = .331, d_z = 0.36 [-0.08, 0.78]; 15 participants were higher during Ad_A and 6 during Ad_B (Wilcoxon V = 163.5, p = .099). Brow Furrow was absent in both ads for 17 of 22 participants; the difference of 0.26 points [-0.47, 0.99], t(21) = 0.75, p = .464, Holm p = .464, d_z = 0.16 [-0.26, 0.58], rests on five participants with any difference (Wilcoxon V = 10, p = .590), and the differences were far from normal (Shapiro Wilk W = 0.646, p < .001), so the rank based test is the appropriate guide. The point estimates favored Ad_A for Joy and Engagement, but each interval includes both zero and an effect of moderate size.

**Q6: Head to head comparisons.** Liking was higher for Ad_A by 0.55 scale points, 95% CI [-0.13, 1.23], t(21) = 1.67, p = .110, Holm p = .331, d_z = 0.36, 95% CI [-0.08, 0.78]; 13 participants preferred Ad_A, 7 Ad_B, and 2 rated both equally (Wilcoxon V = 145.5, p = .124). Brand recall was identical, 12 of 22 (54.5%) for each ad; 5 participants recalled only the ALTAVO brand, 5 only the BRENLOW brand, 7 both, and 5 neither, exact McNemar p = 1.000, Holm p = 1.000, difference = 0.0 percentage points, 95% CI [-28.2, 28.2]. Among the 21 participants with valid GSR during both ads, peaks per minute were 7.05 (SD = 6.89) during Ad_A and 6.00 (SD = 4.94) during Ad_B, difference = 1.05, 95% CI [-1.10, 3.19], t(20) = 1.02, p = .321, Holm p = .642, d_z = 0.22 [-0.21, 0.65] (Figure 3). Purchase intention was measured once, for the product category, so the ads could not be compared on it. (The liking test and the Engagement test of Q5 give nearly the same t, 1.67, and d_z, 0.36, by coincidence; they use different variables.)

**Order checks.** The counterbalancing produced two groups of 11. No order or position effect was significant after Holm's adjustment over the eight checks. Liking did not depend on the order group (AB minus BA = -0.55 scale points, 95% CI [-1.52, 0.43], t(20.00) = -1.16, p = .258, Holm p = 1.000) or on position (first minus second = 0.45 points [-0.21, 1.12], t(20.00) = 1.42, p = .170, Holm p = 1.000). The position effects on dwell were small and not significant (Logo: 1.01 points [-0.45, 2.46], p = .170; Product: 0.51 points [-3.60, 4.62], p = .798; Headline: -0.71 points [-4.26, 2.85], p = .683; all Holm p = 1.000). One check reached p < .05 before adjustment: the BA group dwelled longer on the Headline in both ads (AB minus BA = -5.33 points, 95% CI [-9.73, -0.94], t(20.00) = -2.53, p = .020), but not after it (Holm p = .159). Because the difference appeared in both ads rather than in the ad seen first, it reflects the two groups of 11 rather than the order of presentation, and it cannot bias the comparison between the ads, which is made within participants.

**Exploratory correlations.** Twenty-one Spearman correlations among seven participant level measures were estimated (Figure 4, `SIMULATED_correlations.csv`); if no measure were related to any other, about 1.05 of 21 intervals would exclude zero by chance alone. One interval excluded zero, the correlation between the two category level survey measures, purchase intention and attitude toward reusable water bottles, ρ = .57, 95% CI [.16, .81], which reflects shared content rather than a cross modality relation. The largest cross modality estimates were between dwell on the Logo and the number of correctly recalled brands, ρ = .42, 95% CI [-.02, .73], between Joy and liking, ρ = .33 [-.12, .66], and between liking and recall, ρ = .37 [-.07, .69]; GSR change from Baseline was essentially unrelated to Joy, ρ = -.06 [-.48, .38]. Every interval was wide enough to include both zero and a moderate to large correlation. These estimates are offered as hypotheses for a new study, not as findings.

**Sensitivity power analysis.** With 22 paired observations, a two tailed paired t test at alpha = .05 had 80 percent power only for effects of d_z = 0.63 or larger; its power was .27 for d_z = 0.30 and .61 for d_z = 0.50. Eighty percent power requires 90 participants for d_z = 0.30 and 34 for d_z = 0.50, and 120 and 45 participants, respectively, at the strictest step of a Holm family of three tests (alpha = .05 / 3). For H1, the observed odds ratio of 4.34 with a recall rate of .35 when the Logo was not fixated implies a recall rate of .70 when it was; the standard two proportion formula (normal approximation, equal groups) requires 31 observations per group, 62 in total, and a simulation of Fisher's exact test with the observed share of fixated observations (4,000 simulated studies per sample size) gave a power of .57 at the 44 observations analyzed and reached .81 at 72 independent observations. For orientation, a correlation the size of the standardized H2 slope (.24) requires about 131 participants.

## (c) Discussion

**What the results mean.** Three results are robust within these data. First, both ads raised electrodermal arousal above a resting baseline by large standardized amounts (d_z = 0.83 and 0.95), which shows that the ads were engaging enough to produce a physiological response at all. Second, attention followed a steep hierarchy that did not differ between the ads: the Product held about two fifths of each ad, the Headline about a fifth, and the Logo less than 2 percent, with 10 of 22 participants never looking at the Logo of a given ad. Third, viewers who fixated the Logo were more likely to name the advertised brand afterward (H1), a within person association in the direction of earlier eye tracking evidence that fixations on the brand element of an advertisement contribute to memory for the brand (Wedel & Pieters, 2000), and the exploratory between person correlation of Logo dwell with recall pointed the same way. The comparisons between the ads produced no significant difference on any measure. The point estimates favored the ALTAVO ad on Joy, Engagement, and liking (d_z between 0.31 and 0.36), but these estimates lie well below the smallest effect the design could detect (d_z = 0.63), and their intervals include zero. The appropriate conclusion is therefore that the study cannot tell whether the two ads differ, not that they perform equally; a claim of equivalence would require a formal equivalence test against a smallest effect of interest specified in advance (Lakens et al., 2018) and a far larger sample. Across modalities, the measures converged only weakly at the participant level; with 22 participants, even a correlation of .42 (Logo dwell and recall) could not be distinguished from zero, which underlines why each modality was analyzed with its own planned test rather than combined into a single index.

**Limitations.** The data are simulated, and the results describe a teaching scenario rather than consumer behavior. The sample of 22 university students limits both power and generalizability; the sensitivity analysis shows that only large within person effects could have been detected. Only one ad per brand was tested, so any difference between the ads cannot be separated from the particular executions, and the results do not generalize to other ads of the same brands; treating ads as a random factor requires many ads (Judd et al., 2012; Westfall et al., 2014). The study measured many outcomes across four modalities, and although each family was corrected, the number of families itself raises the chance that some result is a false positive; the planned structure and the exploratory label of the correlation matrix are the safeguards. Interpretation of the biometric measures is limited by reverse inference: a percentage of time with an AFFDEX Joy score at or above 50 records a facial movement classified as a smile, not a felt emotion (Barrett et al., 2019), and GSR peaks index arousal without indicating whether it is positive or negative; inferring a mental state from a physiological measure is valid only to the extent that the measure is specific to that state (Poldrack, 2006; Plassmann et al., 2015). Dwell percentages on the three AOIs are shares of the same 30 s and are therefore not independent; this explains the singular fit of the Q4 model and the unequal variances across AOIs, and it is why the paired comparisons, rather than the model based intervals, were interpreted. Facial expression measures piled up at zero, which makes means and t tests fragile for Brow Furrow in particular. Purchase intention was asked once and about the product category, so no conclusion about the effect of either ad on intention is possible. Finally, Q4 to Q6 were specified after data collection, although before analysis, and should be treated as less confirmatory than H1 to H3.

**What the next study needs.** The power analysis gives concrete targets. To detect a moderate difference between two ads on a paired measure (d_z = 0.50) with 80 percent power, the next study needs at least 34 participants for a single test and 45 if three measures are corrected as a Holm family; a small difference (d_z = 0.30) needs 90 and 120 participants, respectively. H1 needs about 72 independent observations, which means planning the number of people from one ad per person or from a simulation of the mixed model, not from two observations per person. The next study should also present several ads per brand so that brand effects can be separated from executions, ask purchase intention separately for each brand so that the ads can be compared on it, fix every question and its test in a preregistration before data collection (Nosek et al., 2018), and name in advance the smallest difference that would matter to the client, so that a null result can be tested for equivalence rather than left uninterpretable.
