# Model paragraphs for the remote study write up

**SIMULATED DATA.** The paragraphs below are models for students. Every statistic in them comes from the files `SIMULATED_session_data.csv`, `SIMULATED_quality.csv`, `SIMULATED_fea_metrics.csv`, `SIMULATED_aoi_metrics.csv`, and `SIMULATED_survey.csv`, which `make_simulated_data.py` generated (seed 20276200) in the style of the iMotions vendor demo "(DEMO) Food Waste_webET_FEA_voice". No real person was recorded, no webcam was switched on, and the numbers must never be quoted as findings. Nothing from the demo's respondents was used. The paragraphs show the form: what belongs in a methods paragraph for a remote study with webcam eye tracking and webcam facial expression analysis, how to report a recruitment funnel, attrition, and exclusions, how to report a within subjects test, a rank based test with follow up comparisons, and a randomization check in APA style (M, SD, test statistic, df, p, and an effect size with its confidence interval), and how to write a discussion that names the limits of a remote study. Statistics were computed with `analysis.R` (R 4.3.3; effect sizes and their noncentral t intervals were computed by hand and cross-checked with the effectsize package); `analysis_output.txt` holds the full output, and every number below is copied from it (p values are rounded to three decimals).

**Parts of the pipeline are modeled and must be verified on the lab computer before real data are analyzed.** The session data columns Id, Started, Ended, Eye tracking, Est. face %, Block, and Status are the columns of the RDC respondent list, and the funnel step names are those of the iMotions Respondent funnel, but the CSV layout of the session download, the per session funnel step, device, and browser columns, the per slide quality table, the FEA Metrics Export header, and the survey export are modeled. The assignment of each video order to its own study flow, the step at which a phone is stopped, and the status of a session that stops early are also modeled (see the list at the end).

## (a) Methods: data analysis

The study was built in iMotions Lab 11.1 as a Remote Data Collection (RDC) study with the sensors Eye tracking (Web Camera) and Respondent camera, uploaded to my.imotions.com, and run through study links from 2027-03-01 to 2027-03-12; RDC participation requires a desktop or laptop computer. Each order of the two videos was its own study flow (Study flow 1, foodwaste_narrative first; Study flow 2, foodwaste_visual first), and the invitation list was split at random into two halves, each of which received the link of one flow, so the order of every session is recorded in the Block column of the session data. After the consent screen and the setup steps of the respondent funnel (camera, screen size, full screen, and head positioning; screen recording and microphone recording were off, and skipping the positioning check after a timeout was not allowed), respondents completed a 13 point Pre-study calibration, a 30 s instruction slide, both videos in the order of their flow (foodwaste_narrative, 60 s; foodwaste_visual, 62 s), an infographic with four tips to reduce food waste (15 s), an iMotions survey, and a 13 point Post-study calibration, after which their data uploaded to my.imotions.com. The survey asked for the intention to reduce food waste (one item, 1 to 7), which video had been more persuasive (narrative or visual), and the four tips (scored 0 to 4 tips recalled correctly). Completed sessions lasted a median of 8.7 minutes from opening the link to the end of the upload (range 7.2 to 10.2).

After collection the study zip file was downloaded from my.imotions.com and loaded into iMotions Lab, where the face videos were scored with Affectiva AFFDEX (Post Processing > Affectiva, with every stimulus selected) and the FEA thresholding R Notebook was run at a threshold of 50 for Engagement, Joy, and Brow Furrow and of +50 and -50 for Valence, the setting of the vendor's analysis of the Food Waste demo. The FEA Metrics Export supplied, for each respondent and video, the percent of time each expression was above its threshold. AFFDEX Engagement is an expressiveness score derived from a combination of facial action units (Introduction to AFFDEX SDK 5.1); it describes how much the face moved, not attention to the message or persuasion. Webcam gaze on the infographic was analyzed with five rectangular areas of interest (AOIs) on the 1920 x 1080 pixel stimulus: a wide, thin Headline banner (176000 square pixels, 8.49 percent of the screen, the smallest AOI) and four tip boxes of equal size (262400 square pixels, 12.65 percent each), separated by gaps because webcam gaze estimates are off by a few degrees. [Record here the fixation filter shown on the study's GAZE ANALYSIS tab; the help center names Hidden Markov Models as the default for remote studies.] The AOI metrics export supplied dwell time (the summed duration of the fixations inside an AOI, set to 0 ms when the AOI was never fixated), time to first fixation (TTFF), and a hit (at least one fixation inside the AOI); the percent of respondents with a hit is the AOI metric Respondent ratio (%).

Before data collection we fixed an exclusion rule from the thresholds in the iMotions manuals. A completed respondent was excluded when the accuracy (the mean distance between the gaze estimate and the calibration targets, in degrees of visual angle) of the Pre-study or the Post-study calibration was above 4.0 degrees, the upper end of the "generally acceptable range" in the iMotions Online Study Setup guide; when eye tracking quality (the percent of valid gaze samples) on the infographic was below 90 percent; or when face quality (the percent of samples with a face found) was below 80 percent during either video. The same rule applied to every analysis, so all hypotheses were tested on one sample. A sensitivity analysis applied the stricter threshold of 3.0 degrees that the iMotions Online data collection and analysis articles recommend.

The respondent funnel was tabulated from the session data, and completion by order (differential attrition) was tested with a Pearson chi square test without continuity correction, both for all sessions and for the sessions that reached the stimuli, where the Fisher exact test is reported because expected counts fell below 5. Calibration drift was tested with a paired t test of the Post-study against the Pre-study accuracy. H1 was tested with a paired t test of the percent of time Engaged during the two videos, with Cohen's d_z and its 95 percent confidence interval from the noncentral t distribution (Lakens, 2013) and a Wilcoxon signed rank test as a check, because percent of time scores are skewed and often zero. H2 was tested with a Friedman test of the dwell time on the four tips, with Kendall's W as the effect size, followed by Wilcoxon signed rank tests of every pair with Holm's adjustment for six comparisons. H3, a randomization check, compared intention between the two orders with Welch's t test (Delacre et al., 2017), Hedges' g with its noncentral t interval, and a Mann Whitney check. The relation between calibration accuracy and a hit on the smallest AOI was tested with a logistic regression of the Headline hit on the mean of the two accuracies among the completed respondents whose eye tracking and face quality passed, reported as an odds ratio per degree with a profile likelihood interval. Exploratory analyses correlated the Engagement difference (visual minus narrative) with naming the visual video more persuasive (point biserial r) and the total dwell time on the tips with the number of tips recalled (Pearson r and Spearman rho). All tests were two tailed with alpha = .05, and no correction was applied across hypotheses. Analyses used R 4.3.3 with readr, dplyr, tidyr, and ggplot2.

## (b) Results

**Recruitment and attrition.** Of the 260 sessions that opened a study link, 178 (68.5 percent) completed and were processed (Figure 1). Fourteen sessions stopped at the consent screen and 12 at the camera setup, and the largest loss occurred at the screen size step (36 sessions, 34 of them on phones or tablets); none of the 29 phone and 7 tablet sessions completed. In all, 75 sessions (28.8 percent of those that opened a link) stopped before the videos started, 4 stopped during the stimuli (the calibrations, videos, infographic, and questionnaire), and 3 during the upload (2 abandoned, 1 with errors). Completion did not differ by order: 87 of the 126 Narrative first sessions (69.0 percent) and 91 of the 134 Visual first sessions (67.9 percent) completed, χ²(1, N = 260) = 0.04, p = .844, φ = .01. Among the 185 sessions that reached the stimuli, where only the order could have mattered, 87 of 91 (95.6 percent) and 91 of 94 (96.8 percent) completed, Fisher exact p = .718, odds ratio = 0.72, 95% CI [0.10, 4.38].

**Data quality and exclusions.** The exclusion rule removed 36 of the 178 completed respondents (20.2 percent) and kept 142 (69 Narrative first, 73 Visual first). Assigned to the first criterion failed, 5 were excluded for a Pre-study accuracy above 4.0 degrees, 12 for a Post-study accuracy above 4.0 degrees, 14 for eye tracking quality below 90 percent on the infographic, and 5 for face quality below 80 percent during a video; counted without that order, 5, 17, 20, and 14 respondents failed the four criteria, and 14 failed more than one. Exclusion did not depend on order, χ²(1, N = 178) = 0.02, p = .880. Across all completed respondents, accuracy worsened from M = 1.71 degrees (SD = 0.99) at the Pre-study calibration to M = 2.42 degrees (SD = 1.20) at the Post-study calibration, a mean drift of 0.72 degrees (SD = 0.50), 95% CI [0.64, 0.79], t(177) = 19.05, p < .001, d_z = 1.43, 95% CI [1.22, 1.64] (Wilcoxon V = 15415, p < .001). Accuracy was worse at the end for 165 of the 178 respondents (92.7 percent), and the two calibrations correlated strongly (r = .91; Figure 4A). Twelve respondents passed the Pre-study calibration and failed only the Post-study calibration (their accuracy went from M = 3.25 to M = 4.75 degrees), and 6 respondents with a calibration above 4.0 degrees showed 4.0 or less in the rounded session level accuracy of the respondent list. In the kept sample, eye tracking quality on the infographic averaged 96.50 percent (SD = 2.19), and face quality averaged 96.52 percent (SD = 3.52) during the narrative video and 96.61 percent (SD = 3.48) during the visual video. The 142 kept respondents (75 female, 65 male, and 2 who preferred not to say; age M = 29.9, SD = 13.2, range 18 to 64) took part on laptops (99) or desktop computers (43).

**Engagement during the two videos (H1).** Respondents were Engaged (AFFDEX Engagement above 50) for M = 19.49 percent of the narrative video (SD = 19.97, Mdn = 13.22) and M = 43.63 percent of the visual video (SD = 26.39, Mdn = 42.96; Figure 2). The difference of 24.14 percentage points (SD = 28.83), 95% CI [19.36, 28.92], was significant, t(141) = 9.98, p < .001, d_z = 0.84, 95% CI [0.64, 1.03]. Because percent of time scores pile up at zero (21 respondents, 14.8 percent, were never above the threshold during the narrative video) and are right skewed, the Wilcoxon signed rank test served as a check, and it agreed, V = 8852, p < .001; 113 of the 142 respondents were Engaged longer during the visual video. H1 was supported. Descriptively, the visual video also showed more Brow Furrow (M = 8.65 against 4.32 percent of the time), Negative Valence (7.69 against 2.39), Positive Valence (6.65 against 3.09), and Joy (3.86 against 2.08); no hypotheses were tested for these expressions.

**Dwell time on the four tips (H2).** During the 15 s infographic the tips drew a mean dwell time of 3742 ms (Tip_1, SD = 1660), 2100 ms (Tip_2, SD = 1421), 2032 ms (Tip_3, SD = 1239), and 2003 ms (Tip_4, SD = 1247; Figure 3). Dwell time differed among the tips, Friedman χ²(3) = 78.95, p < .001, Kendall's W = .185, n = 142. Tip_1 had the highest mean rank (3.33, against 2.24, 2.27, and 2.16) and the longest dwell time for 83 of the 142 respondents (58.5 percent). Holm adjusted Wilcoxon signed rank tests showed that Tip_1 drew more dwell time than each of the other three tips (mean differences of 1643 to 1739 ms, d_z = 0.66 to 0.73, all adjusted p < .001), whereas Tip_2, Tip_3, and Tip_4 did not differ from one another (mean differences of 29 to 96 ms, all adjusted p = 1.000). H2 was supported, with three of the six pairs different. Nearly every respondent fixated each tip (hit ratios of 98.6 to 100 percent), and the median TTFF followed the reading order (Tip_1 800.5 ms, Tip_2 2077.5 ms, Tip_3 3474 ms, Tip_4 4206 ms).

**Intention by order (H3).** Intention to reduce food waste was M = 5.04 (SD = 1.37, n = 69) after the Narrative first order and M = 5.34 (SD = 1.22, n = 73) after the Visual first order, a difference of -0.30, 95% CI [-0.73, 0.13], that was not significant, Welch t(135.97) = -1.37, p = .171, Hedges' g = -0.23, 95% CI [-0.56, 0.10]; the Mann Whitney test agreed, W = 2243, p = .249. The result is consistent with H3 and is what randomization predicts. A nonsignificant randomization check does not show that the orders were equivalent: the interval still allows a small difference in either direction.

**Calibration accuracy and the smallest AOI.** The Headline banner, the smallest AOI, was fixated by 76.8 percent of the kept respondents, against 98.6 to 100 percent for the tips. Among the 151 completed respondents whose eye tracking and face quality passed (9 of whom were excluded for accuracy only), the odds of a Headline hit fell as the mean calibration accuracy worsened, b = -0.495, SE = 0.200, z = -2.48, p = .013, odds ratio per degree = 0.61, 95% CI [0.40, 0.89]; the predicted hit probability was .84 at 1 degree and .54 at 4 degrees (Figure 4B). The Headline hit ratio was 90.0 percent at a mean accuracy of 1.5 degrees or less (n = 60), 67.7 percent at 1.5 to 2.5 degrees (n = 62), and 63.6 percent at 2.5 to 3.5 degrees (n = 22); the seven respondents above 3.5 degrees were too few for a stable estimate (71.4 percent). In every accuracy band at least 95.5 percent of the respondents hit all four tips.

**Sensitivity analysis.** The stricter 3.0 degree threshold removed a further 17 respondents (125 kept: 59 Narrative first, 66 Visual first) and changed no conclusion. For H1 the difference was 24.36 percentage points, 95% CI [19.47, 29.24], t(124) = 9.87, p < .001, d_z = 0.88, 95% CI [0.67, 1.09] (V = 6949, p < .001). For H2, Friedman χ²(3) = 73.18, p < .001, W = .195, with the same three pairs significant (Tip_1 against each other tip, adjusted p < .001; the other pairs adjusted p = 1.000). For H3 the difference stayed nonsignificant, Welch t(117.79) = -1.10, p = .275, g = -0.20, 95% CI [-0.55, 0.15].

**Exploratory analyses.** The 84 respondents (59.2 percent) who named the visual video more persuasive had a larger Engagement difference (visual minus narrative: M = 30.15 percentage points, SD = 26.18) than the 58 who named the narrative video (M = 15.44, SD = 30.47), point biserial r(140) = .25, 95% CI [.09, .40], p = .003 (Spearman rho = .23, p = .006). Total dwell time on the four tips was only weakly related to the number of tips recalled (M = 2.12 of 4, SD = 1.34), r(140) = .16, 95% CI [-.01, .32], p = .058 (Spearman rho = .16, p = .056). Both are correlations without a hypothesis and describe the sample; they do not show causes.

**Assumption checks.** The H1 differences were close to normal (Shapiro Wilk p = .105, skewness -0.32), although the narrative Engagement scores were right skewed (skewness 1.20), and the drift differences showed no departure from normality (p = .912). The intention ratings, a single 1 to 7 item, departed from normality in both orders (p < .001), which is why the Mann Whitney test accompanies the Welch test; the two agreed. (All values simulated.)

## (c) Discussion

In these simulated data the emotional visual video kept the respondents' faces expressive for more than twice as much of its running time as the narrative video, a large within subjects effect (d_z = 0.84) that held under the stricter accuracy threshold. The direction matches the vendor's analysis of the Food Waste demo, which reported engagement 57 percent of the time for the visual video against 19 percent for the narrative video. Facial expressions recorded through viewers' own webcams have been used to measure responses to advertising at scale (McDuff et al., 2014, 2015; Teixeira et al., 2014), and expressions of emotion during internet video advertisements have been linked to whether viewers keep watching (Teixeira et al., 2012). Engagement here is an expressiveness score, however, not a measure of persuasion; respondents with a larger Engagement difference were somewhat more likely to name the visual video more persuasive (r = .25), but intention to reduce food waste was not measured separately for each video, so the study cannot say which video persuaded more. On the infographic, the top left tip drew the most dwell time and the other three tips did not differ; because Tip_1 is also the first tip in reading order, its position and its content cannot be separated. The randomization check found no order difference in intention, and attrition did not differ by order.

The data quality results are the main lesson of a remote study. More than a quarter of the sessions that opened a link (28.8 percent) ended before the videos started, mostly on phones and tablets, which RDC does not support; a fifth of the completed respondents (20.2 percent) failed the quality rule; and accuracy worsened by 0.72 degrees between the two calibrations, so that 12 respondents who calibrated well at the start no longer met the rule at the end. A study with only a Pre-study calibration would have kept them, and a screen based only on the rounded session level accuracy would have missed 6 failures.

Several limitations are typical of remote studies and would apply to a real version of this one. First, the data are simulated for teaching and were generated to follow the planned effects described in `make_simulated_data.py`; they demonstrate the analysis, not the effects. Second, webcam accuracy limits what an AOI can show. The kept respondents' accuracy averaged about 1.4 degrees at the start and 2 degrees at the end, far from the sub-degree accuracy of an infrared eye tracker (the iMotions Online introduction says the webcam algorithm "does not have the sub-degree accuracy of a conventional infrared eye tracker"); browser based webcam eye tracking is feasible but coarser than laboratory eye tracking (Papoutsaki et al., 2016; Semmelmann & Weigelt, 2018), newer systems come closer to laboratory standards (Kaduk et al., 2024), and accuracy differs between algorithms (Saxena et al., 2024). Only large AOIs with gaps between them give dependable results; the thin Headline banner lost hits as accuracy worsened while the large tips were hit by nearly everyone, and an error large enough to miss one AOI can also place a fixation in its neighbor. The fixation filter applied to the remote data must be recorded. Third, the sample was self selected: people who received an invitation, owned a computer with a webcam, and agreed to be filmed at home, so the results describe that group, and data quality differs across online recruitment sources (Douglas et al., 2023; Peer et al., 2022). The survey had no item to detect careless responding (Meade & Craig, 2012). Fourth, attrition and exclusions were large. Attrition did not differ by order here, but selective attrition in online studies can produce false conclusions (Zhou & Fishbach, 2016), so dropout should be reported at every step (Reips, 2002) and the flow of participants, with exclusions by reason, belongs in every report (Appelbaum et al., 2018). Fifth, the viewing conditions at home were not controlled: screen size, viewing distance, light, camera position, and sound varied, while RDC converts gaze to degrees with fixed screen settings (1920 x 1080 pixels, 47.4 by 29.6 cm at 65 cm), and browsers and devices differ in timing precision (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2021). The narrative video depends on its soundtrack, and nothing in the flow confirmed that the sound was on; the audio output check should be switched on for a real study. Sixth, the facial expression scores were computed in iMotions Lab after the download, because iMotions deprecated server side batch processing on June 1, 2026; post processing takes about as long as the videos, every stimulus must be selected, and the threshold of 50 is a convention, not a property of the face. Seventh, a remote study records identifiable face video in respondents' homes and stores it in the cloud and on the lab computer. The consent text must say what is recorded and where it is stored and processed, gaze data can reveal more about a person than the study needs (Kröger et al., 2020), and the videos must be deleted as the consent and the IRB protocol promise. Finally, the study used a single pair of videos that differ in many ways at once (imagery, pace, music, and length), and a single infographic, so the results describe these stimuli; claims about narrative or emotional formats in general require several stimuli of each kind treated as a random factor (Judd et al., 2012).
