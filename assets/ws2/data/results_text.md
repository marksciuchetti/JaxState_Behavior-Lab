# Model paragraphs for the ad test write up

**SIMULATED DATA.** The paragraphs below are models for students. Every statistic in them comes from `SIMULATED_ad_test_AOI_metrics.csv` and `SIMULATED_ad_test_survey.csv`, which `make_simulated_data.py` generated (seed 20268242) in the style of a two commercial test such as the iMotions demonstration study "DEMO - SonyBravia". No real viewer was recorded, and the numbers must never be quoted as findings. The paragraphs show the form: what belongs in a methods paragraph for a within subjects AOI analysis, how to report paired results in APA style (M, SD, t, df, p, d_z with its confidence interval), how to report a mixed model order check, and how to write a discussion that names the limitations of a screen based eye tracking study. Statistics were computed with `analysis.R` (R 4.3.3, lme4 1.1-35, lmerTest 3.1-3, effectsize 0.8.6); `analysis_output.txt` holds the full output. Every difference is Paint minus Balls, so a negative difference means that the Paint value was lower.

---

## (a) Methods: data analysis

Each respondent (N = 30) viewed two 60 s television commercials for the same brand, Balls and Paint, on a monitor with a Smart Eye Aurora eye tracker sampling at 60 Hz, recorded in iMotions Lab 11.1. A Baseline slide preceded each commercial and a rating survey slide followed it. Viewing order was counterbalanced (Group A saw Balls first, n = 15; Group B saw Paint first, n = 15). Four dynamic areas of interest (AOIs) were drawn on each commercial, each active only while its element was on screen: Product_TV (the television set in the product shots; active for 14 s in Balls and 20 s in Paint), Brand_Logo (the end card logo; the last 5 s), Tagline_Text (the last 4 s), and Hero_Element (the bouncing balls or the paint explosion; 40 s in Balls and 32 s in Paint). Fixation based AOI metrics were exported per respondent and commercial (Individual AOI metrics export, dwell classification threshold 100 ms). The primary dependent variable (H1) was Dwell time (%) on Product_TV, which iMotions expresses relative to the time the AOI was active. H2 concerned the time to first fixation on Brand_Logo (TTFF AOI, in ms from the start of the end card), and H3 concerned liking of each commercial on a scale from 1 to 7. Before inspecting the data we fixed two rules: respondents with Valid data below 70 percent were excluded, and an AOI that a respondent never fixated was treated as TTFF not observed and dwell time zero. One respondent (R23, Group A) was excluded under the first rule (Valid data 58 percent; calibration stayed Poor after two recalibrations), leaving 29 respondents (14 in Group A and 15 in Group B). Each hypothesis was tested with a two tailed paired t test on the Paint minus Balls differences, and the effect size was expressed as Cohen's d_z (the mean difference divided by the SD of the differences) with a 95 percent confidence interval from the noncentral t distribution. A TTFF cannot be paired when a respondent never fixated the logo in one of the commercials, so H2 used the respondents with a TTFF in both commercials, and a sensitivity analysis (H2b) kept every respondent by assigning a never fixated logo the AOI's active duration (5000 ms). Each t test was repeated as a Wilcoxon signed rank test, and the normality of the differences was checked with Shapiro Wilk tests and QQ plots. Because viewing the first commercial could carry over to the second, Product_TV dwell share was also modeled with a linear mixed effects model with commercial, order position (first or second viewing), and their interaction as fixed effects (each coded -0.5 and +0.5) and a random intercept for respondent (lme4), with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (lmerTest); the intraclass correlation (ICC) was computed from the variance components. Brand recall (Yes or No) was related to each respondent's total Brand_Logo dwell time over both commercials with a point biserial correlation (Pearson r with recall coded 0 and 1). All tests were two tailed with alpha = .05, no correction was applied across the three hypotheses, and all numbers reported here are simulated for teaching.

## (b) Results

**Product attention (H1).** Viewers spent a larger share of the time the television set was on screen fixating it in Paint than in Balls. Dwell time (%) on Product_TV was higher for Paint (M = 28.49%, SD = 7.75) than for Balls (M = 21.24%, SD = 6.62), n = 29, a mean difference of 7.25 percentage points, 95% CI [4.43, 10.06], paired t(28) = 5.27, p < .001, d_z = 0.98, 95% CI [0.53, 1.42]. A Wilcoxon signed rank test agreed (V = 402, p < .001), and the differences showed no departure from normality (Shapiro Wilk p = .970). A respondent's two dwell shares were positively correlated (r = .48), which is the source of the power of the paired design.

**Order effect.** In the mixed model of Product_TV dwell share, the commercial effect was b = 7.37, SE = 1.21, t(27) = 6.07, p < .001, 95% CI [4.88, 9.86]. Order position also had an effect: the commercial seen second drew 3.61 percentage points more Product_TV dwell share than the same commercial seen first, SE = 1.21, t(27) = 2.97, p = .006, 95% CI [1.12, 6.10] (cell means: Balls 19.86% when seen first and 22.54% when seen second; Paint 26.29% first and 30.84% second). The commercial by order position interaction did not reach significance, b = 1.87, SE = 4.67, t(27) = 0.40, p = .691, 95% CI [-7.71, 11.45]; it is a comparison between the two order groups and therefore has a wide interval. The random intercept for respondent (SD = 5.36 percentage points, residual SD = 4.62) gave an ICC of .574 after adjustment for the fixed effects (.185 in a model without them), so more than half of the variance that remained lay between viewers.

**Brand logo (H2).** The logo was fixated at least once by 27 of 29 respondents (93.1%) in each commercial (exact McNemar test of the two respondent ratios, p = 1.000), and 25 respondents had a TTFF in both commercials. Among these 25, the time to first fixation on Brand_Logo was shorter for Paint (M = 786.96 ms, SD = 192.27) than for Balls (M = 906.76 ms, SD = 242.15), a mean difference of -119.80 ms, 95% CI [-256.30, 16.70], but the difference did not reach significance, paired t(24) = -1.81, p = .083, d_z = -0.36, 95% CI [-0.76, 0.05]. The Wilcoxon signed rank test gave V = 88, p = .046, so the two tests fell on opposite sides of .05 and the conclusion should be stated as uncertain. When the four respondents with a missing TTFF were kept by assigning 5000 ms to the commercial in which they never fixated the logo, the mean difference was similar in size but far less precise, -124.24 ms, 95% CI [-746.53, 498.05], t(28) = -0.41, p = .686, d_z = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.44, 0.29] (Wilcoxon V = 142, p = .105), so the result depends on how never fixated logos are handled.

**Liking (H3).** Liking was higher for Balls (M = 5.52, SD = 0.91) than for Paint (M = 5.00, SD = 1.13), a mean difference of -0.52 points, 95% CI [-0.93, -0.10], paired t(28) = -2.56, p = .016, d_z = -0.47, 95% CI [-0.86, -0.09]; the Wilcoxon signed rank test agreed (V = 9, p = .017). Ten respondents rated Balls higher, 2 rated Paint higher, and 17 gave the two commercials the same rating. When forced to choose, 22 of 29 respondents (75.9%) preferred Balls, 95% CI [56.5%, 89.7%], exact binomial p = .008. Attention and liking pointed in opposite directions: Paint drew the larger Product_TV dwell share for 25 of 29 respondents, yet Balls was the better liked commercial, and the respondent level difference in product dwell share was unrelated to the difference in liking, r = .04, 95% CI [-.33, .40], p = .844 (exploratory).

**Brand recall.** Brand recall was Yes for 24 of 29 respondents (82.8%). Respondents who recalled the brand had a longer total Brand_Logo dwell time over the two commercials (M = 4124 ms, SD = 1387, n = 24) than respondents who did not (M = 2532 ms, SD = 1195, n = 5), r_pb(27) = .42, 95% CI [.06, .68], p = .025; a rank based check agreed (Wilcoxon rank sum W = 21, p = .026).

**Assumption checks.** The paired differences for H1 (Shapiro Wilk p = .970) and H2 (p = .676) showed no departure from normality. The H3 differences are whole numbers on a 7 point scale and departed from normality (p < .001), which is why the Wilcoxon result is reported beside the t test. The mixed model residuals (p = .806) and the respondent intercepts (p = .976) showed no departure from normality. (All values simulated.)

## (c) Discussion

In these simulated data, the two commercials separated attention from attitude. Paint held the viewers' eyes on the television set for a larger share of the time the set was on screen (about seven percentage points, a large paired effect), yet Balls was the better liked and the more often preferred commercial, and the respondents who gave Paint more product attention were no more likely to like it. Dwell time shows where attention went, not how the viewer felt about what was seen, so the eye tracking result needs a criterion measure such as recall, stated intent, or choice before it is read as an advantage for the brand. Brand recall was higher among respondents who looked longer at the logo, but the association is correlational and rests on only five respondents who did not recall the brand. The order effect of about 3.6 percentage points shows why a within subjects test of commercials must be counterbalanced: because about half of the viewers saw each commercial first, the carryover averaged out of the commercial comparison, whereas a fixed order would have added it to the commercial seen second and overstated its advantage.

Several limitations are typical of screen based eye tracking of commercials and would apply to a real version of this study. First, every dynamic AOI metric depends on the timeline the analyst drew: the active intervals, the key frames that follow a moving element, and the size and padding of the shape. Dwell time (%) is relative to the active time, so AOIs that are on screen for 14 s and 20 s are comparable in share but not in seconds, and a short active interval gives a noisier share; the AOI definitions should be reported with the results. Second, a 60 Hz tracker records one gaze sample every 16.7 ms, so TTFF and short fixations on a small AOI such as the logo are coarse and sensitive to calibration accuracy; the Valid data metric, the calibration results, and the exclusion rule belong in the report, and the one exclusion here (R23, calibration Poor) shows how tracking quality limits what can be analyzed. Third, TTFF has missing values by definition. Dropping respondents who never fixated the logo (H2) and coding them with the longest possible value (H2b) answer different questions and gave results of different precision; the iMotions TTFF max. metric offers a third coding (it counts from the start of the commercial and assigns the commercial's duration), and the report should say which was used. Fourth, the design compares two commercials, so any difference combines story, music, length of the product shots, and the layout of the end card, and it cannot be generalized to other commercials without a larger sample of stimuli. Fifth, the sample is small (29 respondents), three hypotheses were tested without correction, and the two H2 tests lie on either side of the .05 line, so a replication is needed before any practical recommendation. Sixth, respondents in a laboratory watch the commercials without distraction and know they are observed, which may inflate attention to the logo and product relative to a living room, and the rating slide after each commercial may draw the viewer's attention to what was just shown. Finally, these numbers are simulated for teaching and were generated to follow the planned effects described in `make_simulated_data.py`; they demonstrate the analysis, not the effect.
