# Model paragraphs for the GSR and heart rate write up

**SIMULATED DATA.** The paragraphs below are models for students. Every statistic in them comes from the files `SIMULATED_gsr_peak_metrics.csv`, `SIMULATED_gsr_signal_quality.csv`, `SIMULATED_gsr_peak_table.csv`, `SIMULATED_heart_rate.csv`, and `SIMULATED_gsr_survey.csv`, which `make_simulated_data.py` generated (seed 20278504) in the style of a two commercial arousal test such as the lab's library studies "Nike Campaign Ad Evaluation" and "e.l.f. Campaign Ad Evaluation". No real person was recorded, and the numbers must never be quoted as findings. Nothing about the content of either commercial was used. The paragraphs show the form: what belongs in a methods paragraph for a skin conductance and heart rate study with two Shimmer3 GSR+ units, how to report paired results in APA style (M, SD, t, df, p, d_z with its confidence interval), how to report a mixed model order check and a sensitivity analysis for nonresponders, and how to write a discussion that names the limits of a GSR study. Statistics were computed with `analysis.R` (R 4.3.3, lme4 1.1.35.1, lmerTest 3.1.3, effectsize 0.8.6); `analysis_output.txt` holds the full output, and every number below is copied from it (p values are rounded to three decimals, and values below .001 are written p < .001). Differences for H1 and H3 are commercial minus its own baseline. The difference for H2 is Nike minus e.l.f.

**Two parts of the pipeline are modeled and must be verified on the lab machine before real data are analyzed.** First, the manuals name no PPG heart rate column or R Notebook, so the heart rate column `Mean PPG Heart Rate (BPM)` is a modeled name for the mean of the beats per minute value that iMotions displays for the PPG channel. Second, the layout of the GSR Signal Quality export is modeled from the manual's description (Signal-to-Noise ratio in decibels and Signal missing in percent per stimulus and respondent).

## (a) Methods: data analysis

Each respondent (N = 30) watched two television commercials on a monitor in the lab, one for Nike (60 s) and one for e.l.f. (30 s), while wearing a Shimmer3 GSR+ unit on the non-dominant hand (skin conductance electrodes on two fingers, the optical pulse probe on a free finger), recorded in iMotions Lab 11.1. Respondents were run two at a time, one at each of two seats, each with its own Shimmer3 GSR+ unit; the units were recorded as separate flows and pooled in the analysis (Multiple device aggregation). Skin conductance was recorded at 128 Hz, the rate the iMotions Shimmer setup article calls sufficient for GSR, and the planned warm up on the sensor before the first sequence was 5 minutes (a lab rule, because the manuals state no warm up time; the session log records the actual warm up, 2 to 8 minutes). Each sequence consisted of a 60 s Baseline, the commercial, a 15 s Recovery, and a short questionnaire (self reported arousal on a 1 to 9 self assessment manikin style scale and liking on a 1 to 7 scale), and a rest of 2 minutes separated the two sequences. Viewing order was counterbalanced (Group A saw Nike first, n = 15; Group B saw e.l.f. first, n = 15). Skin conductance was processed with the iMotions GSR Peak Detection R Notebook, which uses the algorithm of Benedek and Kaernbach (2010) [record here the parameter values shown in the notebook's parameter panel: gap interpolation length threshold, phasic filter length, lowpass filter cutoff, peak onset threshold, peak offset threshold, peak amplitude threshold, and minimum peak duration], and the GSR Peak Metrics export supplied, per respondent and stimulus, Has Peaks, Peak Count, Peaks Per Minute (Peak Count divided by Signal Duration in minutes, where Signal Duration leaves out gaps longer than 4 s), and Average Peak Amplitude (microSiemens). Signal quality was taken from the GSR Signal Quality R Notebook (Signal-to-Noise ratio in dB and the percentage of signal missing per stimulus and respondent). Heart rate was the mean of the PPG beats per minute value over each stimulus; because heart rate variability needs an ECG signal and the lab has none, only mean heart rate was analyzed.

The primary dependent variable (H1) was Peaks Per Minute in each commercial compared with the Baseline that preceded it. H2 compared Peaks Per Minute between the two commercials, and H3 concerned the change in mean heart rate (commercial minus baseline) for each commercial and the difference between the two changes. The Recovery windows last only 15 s, so Peaks Per Minute there moves in steps of 4 and is reported descriptively only. Before inspecting the data we fixed two rules. The first was an exclusion rule: a respondent was excluded when the Signal-to-Noise ratio was below 0 dB (which the iMotions help article calls poor) or more than 20 percent of the signal was missing (the lab's own threshold) in either commercial. The second was a definition: a nonresponder is a respondent with Has Peaks = 0 in at least 4 of the 6 stimuli; nonresponders stay in the main analysis because the absence of phasic activity is a finding, not a recording fault, and they are removed in a sensitivity analysis. Two respondents were excluded under the first rule (P11, Signal-to-Noise ratio -3.4 dB in the Nike commercial after the electrode lead loosened; P24, 38.0 percent of the signal missing in the e.l.f. commercial after a Bluetooth dropout), leaving 28 respondents (15 in Group A and 13 in Group B). In the kept data the Signal-to-Noise ratio had M = 23.0 dB (SD = 3.3, range 14.5 to 30.6), and the maximum percentage of signal missing was 8.5. Three of the 28 kept respondents (10.7%) were nonresponders (P03, P16, P21).

Each hypothesis was tested with a two tailed paired t test, and the effect size was expressed as Cohen's d_z (the mean difference divided by the SD of the differences) with a 95 percent confidence interval from the noncentral t distribution. Each t test was repeated as a Wilcoxon signed rank test, and the normality of the differences was checked with Shapiro Wilk tests and QQ plots, because Peaks Per Minute is built from whole peak counts. Because viewing the first commercial could carry over to the second, Peaks Per Minute in the commercials was also modeled with a linear mixed effects model with commercial, order position (first or second viewing), and their interaction as fixed effects (each coded -0.5 and +0.5) and a random intercept for respondent (lme4), with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (lmerTest); the intraclass correlation (ICC) was computed from the variance components. The H1 and H2 tests were repeated without the nonresponders (sensitivity analysis). In an exploratory analysis, self reported arousal in each commercial was correlated with Peaks Per Minute in the same commercial (Pearson r with a Fisher z interval, Spearman rho as a rank based check). The two devices were compared on respondent mean Signal-to-Noise ratio and on respondent mean Peaks Per Minute with Welch t tests before they were pooled. The moment to moment view gives the percentage of respondents with a peak in each 5 s time bin (bins start every 4 s, so successive bins overlap by 1 s), the iMotions GSR Peak Binned Aggregation default, computed from the peak table. All tests were two tailed with alpha = .05, no correction was applied across the three hypotheses, and all numbers reported here are simulated for teaching.

## (b) Results

**Descriptive statistics.** Peaks Per Minute was higher during the commercials than during their baselines (Baseline_Nike M = 2.30, SD = 1.09; Nike_Commercial M = 4.66, SD = 2.04; Baseline_elf M = 2.47, SD = 1.34; elf_Commercial M = 3.50, SD = 1.86; n = 28 in each cell). In the 15 s Recovery windows the values were M = 3.29 (SD = 2.45) after Nike and M = 3.29 (SD = 2.19) after e.l.f. Average Peak Amplitude was 0.182 microSiemens in the Nike baseline and 0.194 in the e.l.f. baseline, 0.274 in the Nike commercial, and 0.254 in the e.l.f. commercial (respondents without a peak have no amplitude). A respondent's Peaks Per Minute in a baseline and in the following commercial were positively correlated (Nike r = .50, e.l.f. r = .57), and so were the two commercials (r = .48), which is the source of the power of the paired design.

**Arousal response (H1).** Viewers had more skin conductance peaks per minute during each commercial than during the baseline before it. For Nike, Peaks Per Minute rose from 2.30 (SD = 1.09) to 4.66 (SD = 2.04), n = 28, a mean difference of 2.36 peaks per minute, 95% CI [1.68, 3.05], paired t(27) = 7.06, p < .001, d_z = 1.33, 95% CI [0.82, 1.84]. A Wilcoxon signed rank test agreed (V = 317, p < .001), although the differences departed from normality (Shapiro Wilk p = .029). Twenty-two of 28 respondents had more peaks per minute in the Nike commercial than in its baseline (3 had fewer and 3 the same), and 27 of 28 (96.4%) had at least one peak during the commercial. For e.l.f., Peaks Per Minute rose from 2.47 (SD = 1.34) to 3.50 (SD = 1.86), a mean difference of 1.03 peaks per minute, 95% CI [0.43, 1.63], t(27) = 3.52, p = .002, d_z = 0.67, 95% CI [0.25, 1.07]; the Wilcoxon signed rank test agreed (V = 190, p = .001), and the differences again departed from normality (Shapiro Wilk p = .014). Fifteen of 28 respondents had more peaks per minute in the e.l.f. commercial than in its baseline (5 had fewer and 8 the same), and 25 of 28 (89.3%) had at least one peak during the commercial.

**Nike versus e.l.f. (H2) and order.** Peaks Per Minute was higher in the Nike commercial (M = 4.66, SD = 2.04) than in the e.l.f. commercial (M = 3.50, SD = 1.86), a mean difference of 1.16 peaks per minute, 95% CI [0.38, 1.93], paired t(27) = 3.07, p = .005, d_z = 0.58, 95% CI [0.17, 0.98]; the Wilcoxon signed rank test agreed (V = 179, p = .006), and the differences showed no departure from normality (Shapiro Wilk p = .144). Nike was higher for 16 of 28 respondents, lower for 4, and equal for 8. In the mixed model of Peaks Per Minute in the commercials, the commercial effect was b = 1.12, SE = 0.37, t(26) = 3.02, p = .006, 95% CI [0.36, 1.88]. Order position did not reach significance: the commercial seen second drew 0.55 fewer peaks per minute than the commercial seen first, b = -0.55, SE = 0.37, t(26) = -1.48, p = .150, 95% CI [-1.31, 0.21]. The simple estimate from the two order groups, ((0.57 - 1.67) / 2 = -0.55, n = 13 and 15), is the same number. The commercial by order position interaction was also not significant, b = 1.92, SE = 1.24, t(26) = 1.55, p = .134, 95% CI [-0.63, 4.47], and its interval is wide because it is a comparison between the two order groups. The raw cell means (Nike 4.47 when seen first and 4.88 when seen second; e.l.f. 4.31 first and 2.80 second) come from different respondents in each cell and therefore mix order with individual differences; the order effect above is the within respondent contrast. The random intercept for respondent (SD = 1.31 peaks per minute, residual SD = 1.38) gave an ICC of .475 after adjustment for the fixed effects (.370 in a model without them), so almost half of the variance that remained lay between respondents.

**Heart rate (H3).** Mean heart rate was lower during each commercial than during its baseline. In the Nike sequence it fell from 76.17 BPM (SD = 8.39) to 74.66 BPM (SD = 8.33), a mean change of -1.51 BPM, 95% CI [-2.25, -0.76], paired t(27) = -4.16, p < .001, d_z = -0.79, 95% CI [-1.21, -0.36] (Wilcoxon V = 44.5, p < .001); 21 of 28 respondents had a lower heart rate in the commercial. In the e.l.f. sequence it fell from 75.43 BPM (SD = 8.81) to 74.46 BPM (SD = 8.34), a mean change of -0.98 BPM, 95% CI [-1.68, -0.27], t(27) = -2.82, p = .009, d_z = -0.53, 95% CI [-0.93, -0.13] (Wilcoxon V = 95.5, p = .015); 17 of 28 respondents had a lower heart rate. The two changes did not differ reliably: the change was 0.53 BPM larger in the Nike sequence, mean difference of the changes = -0.53 BPM (Nike change minus e.l.f. change), 95% CI [-1.46, 0.39], t(27) = -1.18, p = .249, d_z = -0.22, 95% CI [-0.60, 0.15] (Wilcoxon V = 153.5, p = .264). A respondent's heart rate in a baseline and in the following commercial was almost the same (Nike r = .97, e.l.f. r = .98), which is why a change of 1 to 2 BPM can be detected against a between respondent SD of about 8.5 BPM.

**Self reported arousal (exploratory).** Self reported arousal was M = 6.00 (SD = 1.39) for Nike and M = 5.43 (SD = 1.85) for e.l.f. Arousal and Peaks Per Minute in the same commercial were positively related but the intervals include zero: Nike r(26) = .37, 95% CI [-.01, .65], p = .056 (Spearman rho = .33, p = .086), e.l.f. r(26) = .34, 95% CI [-.03, .64], p = .073 (rho = .39, p = .038), and for the respondent means over the two commercials r(26) = .30, 95% CI [-.08, .61], p = .116 (rho = .25, p = .196).

**Sensitivity analysis without the nonresponders.** The three nonresponders had a mean of 0.33 peaks per minute over the four baseline and commercial stimuli, against 3.58 for the 25 responders. Without them, the results kept their direction, the H1 effects became larger, and the H2 effect stayed the same size. Nike: 2.49 (SD = 0.97) to 5.14 (SD = 1.56), mean difference = 2.65, 95% CI [1.97, 3.32], t(24) = 8.11, p < .001, d_z = 1.62, 95% CI [1.01, 2.22] (Wilcoxon V = 272.5, p < .001). e.l.f.: 2.77 (SD = 1.09) to 3.92 (SD = 1.47), mean difference = 1.15, 95% CI [0.49, 1.81], t(24) = 3.61, p = .001, d_z = 0.72, 95% CI [0.28, 1.16] (V = 190, p = .001). Nike versus e.l.f.: mean difference = 1.22, 95% CI [0.35, 2.08], t(24) = 2.89, p = .008, d_z = 0.58, 95% CI [0.15, 1.00] (V = 145, p = .010), with an order estimate of -0.58 ((0.62 - 1.77) / 2, n = 12 and 13). The nonresponders also raised the within respondent correlations; among responders only they were r = .24 (Nike baseline with commercial), r = .25 (e.l.f.), and r = .04 (between the commercials). Among responders the relation with self reported arousal was stronger: Nike r(23) = .50, 95% CI [.13, .75], p = .011, and e.l.f. r(23) = .45, 95% CI [.07, .72], p = .023. The nonresponders' heart rate fell at least as much as that of the others (mean change -2.13 BPM in the Nike sequence and -1.67 in the e.l.f. sequence, against -1.43 and -0.89 for the responders), as expected because PPG is a separate sensor from the skin conductance electrodes. No conclusion changed.

**Why Peaks Per Minute uses Signal Duration, and the devices.** Three kept stimulus rows had a Bluetooth gap longer than 4 s; dividing by Duration instead of Signal Duration would have lowered Peaks Per Minute in those rows by up to 0.415. In the excluded e.l.f. row of P24 the understatement would have been 2.452 (6.452 versus 4.000 peaks per minute). The two devices did not differ in respondent mean Signal-to-Noise ratio (Seat 1 M = 22.90 dB, Seat 2 M = 23.07 dB, Welch t(25.9) = -0.16, p = .876) or in respondent mean Peaks Per Minute over the baselines and commercials (M = 3.34 and M = 3.12, t(17.4) = 0.47, p = .645), so pooling them was reasonable.

**Moment to moment.** In the Nike commercial the mean percentage of respondents with a peak per 5 s bin was 38.5% over 14 complete bins; it was lowest in the first bin (28.6%, 0 to 5 s) and highest at 48 to 53 s (57.1%, 16 of 28 respondents). In the e.l.f. commercial the mean over 7 bins was 29.6%, the first bin was lowest (14.3%), and the highest bin was 12 to 17 s (42.9%, 12 of 28 respondents).

**Assumption checks.** The paired differences for the Peaks Per Minute tests in H1 departed from normality (Shapiro Wilk p = .029 for Nike and p = .014 for e.l.f.), which is why the Wilcoxon result is reported beside each t test; the H2 differences (p = .144) and the H3 differences (Nike p = .112, e.l.f. p = .339, difference of the changes p = .785) showed no departure. The mixed model residuals (p = .166) showed no departure from normality, and the respondent intercepts did (p = .002), which is expected with three nonresponders in the sample. (All values simulated.)

## (c) Discussion

In these simulated data, both commercials raised skin conductance arousal above the viewer's baseline, and the Nike commercial raised it more than the e.l.f. commercial. The H1 effects were large for Nike (d_z = 1.33) and medium to large for e.l.f. (d_z = 0.67), and the difference between the two commercials was medium (d_z = 0.58), so the answer to "which commercial is more arousing" is Nike in this sample. GSR shows the intensity of arousal, not whether the viewer liked what was seen: the same rise could come from enjoyment, surprise, or irritation. Liking was almost the same for the two commercials (Nike M = 5.29, SD = 1.30; e.l.f. M = 5.18, SD = 1.22), and the relation between self reported arousal and Peaks Per Minute was moderate and not reliable in the full sample, so the GSR result needs a valence measure (for example facial expression analysis, the topic of Workshop 3, or a rating) and a criterion measure such as recall or choice before it is read as an advantage for either brand. Heart rate did not separate the commercials as clearly as skin conductance did: it fell by 1.51 BPM in the Nike sequence and 0.98 BPM in the e.l.f. sequence, and the two changes did not differ reliably. A small decline during a commercial can reflect attention, a change in posture, or a gradual settling over the session, and this design cannot tell them apart.

Several limitations are typical of GSR studies of commercials and would apply to a real version of this study. First, the two commercials differ in length (60 s and 30 s), story, and music, so any difference combines these features and cannot be generalized to other commercials without a larger sample of stimuli. Peaks Per Minute is a rate, which makes the lengths comparable, but a 30 s commercial gives a rate that moves in steps of 2 peaks per minute and a 15 s Recovery gives steps of 4, so the shorter windows are coarse and the Recovery results are descriptive only. Second, about one respondent in ten (10.7%) produced almost no phasic activity. The nonresponders are a physiological result, not a recording fault, so they were kept in the main analysis and removed in the sensitivity analysis; they diluted the H1 effect sizes and raised the within respondent correlations (the baseline to commercial correlations were .50 and .57 with them and .24 and .25 without them), which matters for the power of a paired design, and a study should plan for them in the sample size. Third, the order effect was small and not significant, but with 13 and 15 respondents in the two order groups the study has little power to detect carryover, the interaction interval is wide, and both exclusions came from Group B, so the groups are unequal; the 2 minute rest is a design choice, and counterbalancing, not the rest, is what protects the comparison. Fourth, the heart rate values rest on a PPG channel whose calculation method the manuals do not state, no R Notebook for PPG heart rate is named, and the column name in these files is modeled; PPG is also not recommended for heart rate variability, so only mean heart rate was analyzed, and a pilot export on the lab machine must confirm what the channel contains before real data are collected. Fifth, the two Shimmer3 GSR+ units were pooled after a device check on 14 respondents each; a real study should record which unit each respondent wore, keep the unit constant for each seat, and check the Signal-to-Noise ratio of both. Sixth, the exclusion rule (Signal-to-Noise ratio below 0 dB or more than 20 percent of the signal missing in a commercial) was written before collection, and it removed one loose electrode and one Bluetooth dropout; the reasons for each decision belong in the report, as do the Signal-to-Noise values of the kept data (M = 23.0 dB, SD = 3.3), because the manual treats values from 0 to 20 dB as needing a look at the signal and 31 of 168 kept rows fell in that band. Seventh, three kept rows had a Bluetooth gap, which is why Peaks Per Minute divides by Signal Duration; a report should say which denominator was used. Finally, these numbers are simulated for teaching and were generated to follow the planned effects described in `make_simulated_data.py`; they demonstrate the analysis, not the effect.
