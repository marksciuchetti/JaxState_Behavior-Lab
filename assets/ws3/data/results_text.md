# Model paragraphs for the facial expression analysis write up

**SIMULATED DATA.** The paragraphs below are models for students. Every statistic in them comes from `SIMULATED_fea_metrics.csv`, `SIMULATED_fea_survey.csv`, and the two threshold files `SIMULATED_fea_metrics_threshold25.csv` and `SIMULATED_fea_metrics_threshold75.csv`, which `make_simulated_data.py` generated (seed 20265798) in the structure of the lab's library study "Nike Campaign Ad Evaluation" (a Baseline, one commercial, a Recovery period, and a short questionnaire, with the respondent camera recorded for AFFDEX). No real viewer was recorded, nothing is said about what the commercial shows, and the numbers must never be quoted as findings. The paragraphs show the form: what belongs in a methods paragraph for a facial expression analysis of a video commercial, how to report a zero inflated paired comparison in APA style (descriptives, the count of viewers with any expression, a signed rank test beside the paired t test, d_z with its confidence interval), how to report a mixed model across time bins together with the proportion of viewers who show the expression, and how to write a discussion that names the limitations of webcam based facial expression coding. Statistics were computed with `analysis.R` (R 4.3.3, lme4 1.1.35.1, lmerTest 3.1.3, effectsize 0.8.6); `analysis_output.txt` holds the full output. Every H1 difference is Commercial minus Baseline, so a positive difference means more Joy during the commercial.

## (a) Methods: data analysis

Thirty-two respondents (V01 to V32) viewed one 60 s video commercial on a monitor in the lab, each once and in the same order: a 60 s Baseline, the commercial, a 15 s Recovery, and a short questionnaire. The respondent camera (a webcam) recorded each face during the session in iMotions Lab 11.1, and the recordings were post processed with Affectiva AFFDEX (every video frame analyzed). The FEA thresholding R Notebook then converted the AFFDEX scores into present or absent for each frame at its default thresholds (Emotion 50; Valence +50 for Positive Valence and -50 for Negative Valence), so that an expression counted as present in a frame when its score passed the threshold. A binning annotation divided the commercial into six consecutive 10 s bins (Bin_01 to Bin_06). The FEA Metrics Export supplied, for each respondent and each interval (Baseline, Commercial, Recovery, and the six bins), the percent of the interval during which each expression was present (Percent of time present) and the number of separate segments of presence. The expressions analyzed were Joy, Surprise, Brow Furrow, Positive Valence, and Negative Valence; Engagement was not thresholded and is reported as a mean raw score (0 to 100) per interval. Data quality was the percentage of frames successfully analyzed after post processing (the Quality Metrics value), which describes how much of the recording AFFDEX could analyze and not how accurate the coding was. Before inspecting the data we fixed the exclusion rule, a Data quality of at least 80 percent; this rule is the lab's own, because the iMotions manuals state no minimum. Two respondents were excluded under the rule (V07, Data quality 61 percent, glare on the glasses lenses; V19, 72 percent, looked down at a phone during the commercial), leaving 30 respondents (17 women and 13 men; age M = 27.4 years, SD = 5.4, range 19 to 45) whose Data quality averaged 94.8 percent (SD = 2.61, range 87.8 to 98.8). Sessions took place between October 26 and October 29, 2026. The primary dependent variable (H1) was the Percent of time present for Joy, which was zero for most viewers at Baseline. Because of this zero inflation, the primary test of H1 was a Wilcoxon signed rank test on the Commercial minus Baseline differences (normal approximation with continuity and tie corrections), reported with the matched pairs rank biserial correlation, the Hodges-Lehmann median difference, and the number of viewers with any Joy in each period, which was compared with an exact McNemar test. A paired t test and Cohen's d_z (the mean difference divided by the SD of the differences, with a 95 percent confidence interval from the noncentral t distribution) were reported beside it. To judge how much the result depends on the threshold, H1 was repeated after rerunning the notebook at a batch threshold of 25 and of 75 (Valence at +25 and -25, then +75 and -75). H2 concerned Joy across the six bins. A viewer counted as showing Joy in a bin when Percent of time present in that bin was above 0, the quantity that the FEA thresholding Aggregation displays as the proportion of respondents; exact Clopper-Pearson 95 percent confidence intervals were computed for these proportions. Percent of time present was modeled with a linear mixed effects model with bin as a factor and a random intercept for respondent (lme4), with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (lmerTest); the intraclass correlation (ICC) was computed from the variance components. Two planned contrasts compared Bin_05 and Bin_06 with the mean of Bin_01 to Bin_04 (Holm adjustment), and a Friedman test with Kendall's W served as the nonparametric check. H3 related the Percent of time present for Positive Valence during the commercial to the rating of the ad (Ad_liking_1to7) with a Pearson correlation (Fisher z interval) and a Spearman correlation. Shapiro Wilk tests and QQ plots checked the distributions of the differences, the model residuals, and the respondent intercepts. All tests were two tailed with alpha = .05, no correction was applied across the three hypotheses, and all numbers reported here are simulated for teaching.

## (b) Results

**Joy during the commercial (H1).** Viewers showed Joy for a larger share of the commercial than of the Baseline. Joy was present for M = 10.37% of the commercial (SD = 13.57, Mdn = 5.86) and for M = 1.61% of the Baseline (SD = 3.40, Mdn = 0.00), n = 30, a mean difference of 8.76 percentage points, 95% CI [4.34, 13.18]. Joy was absent for the whole Baseline in 22 of 30 viewers, so the data are zero inflated, and the Wilcoxon signed rank test is the primary test: V = 331, p < .001 (26 nonzero differences: 23 positive, 3 negative, and 4 ties at zero), with a matched pairs rank biserial correlation of .89 and a Hodges-Lehmann median difference of 8.36 percentage points, 95% CI [4.48, 13.64]. The paired t test agreed, t(29) = 4.05, p < .001, d_z = 0.74, 95% CI [0.33, 1.14]. Eight of 30 viewers (26.7%) showed any Joy at Baseline and 25 of 30 (83.3%) during the commercial; one viewer showed Joy only at Baseline and 18 only during the commercial, exact McNemar test p < .001. The differences were not normally distributed (Shapiro Wilk W = 0.816, p < .001), which is why the signed rank test is primary. During the 15 s Recovery, Joy was present for M = 3.10% of the time (SD = 7.08, Mdn = 0.00; 7 of 30 viewers).

**Sensitivity to the threshold.** The direction of the result did not depend on the threshold, but the size did. At a threshold of 25, Joy was present for M = 17.76% of the commercial (SD = 19.35) and M = 3.44% of the Baseline (SD = 5.93); the mean difference was 14.32 percentage points, 95% CI [8.35, 20.28], Wilcoxon V = 389, p < .001, t(29) = 4.91, p < .001, d_z = 0.90, 95% CI [0.47, 1.32]; 13 of 30 viewers showed Joy at Baseline and 28 of 30 during the commercial. At a threshold of 75, the means were M = 4.21% (SD = 7.10) and M = 0.56% (SD = 1.43), a difference of 3.65 percentage points, 95% CI [1.28, 6.02], V = 183.5, p < .001 (19 nonzero differences, 11 ties at zero), t(29) = 3.15, p = .004, d_z = 0.57, 95% CI [0.18, 0.96]; 7 of 30 viewers showed Joy at Baseline and 18 of 30 during the commercial, exact McNemar test p = .003. The mean Percent of time present for Joy in the commercial was therefore 17.76%, 10.37%, and 4.21% at thresholds of 25, 50, and 75.

**Joy across the bins (H2).** Joy was concentrated in the last two bins. The mean Percent of time present was 4.83%, 7.73%, 6.42%, 5.41%, 24.36%, and 13.43% in Bin_01 to Bin_06 (SDs 11.59, 15.14, 17.18, 11.84, 28.69, and 21.86), and the proportion of viewers with Joy present was 23.3% (7 of 30), 26.7%, 23.3%, 30.0%, 66.7% (20 of 30, 95% CI [47.2%, 82.7%]), and 53.3% (16 of 30, 95% CI [34.3%, 71.7%]). The bin factor was significant in the mixed model, F(5, 145) = 8.52, p < .001. Relative to the mean of Bin_01 to Bin_04, Joy was higher by 18.26 percentage points in Bin_05, SE = 2.88, t(145) = 6.34, Holm p < .001, 95% CI [12.56, 23.95], and by 7.33 percentage points in Bin_06, SE = 2.88, t(145) = 2.55, Holm p = .012, 95% CI [1.64, 13.03]. The random intercept for respondent (variance = 150.93, residual variance = 199.04) gave an ICC of .431 after adjustment for the bin effect (.364 in a model without it), so viewers differed substantially in how much Joy they showed. The Friedman test agreed, chi-squared(5) = 34.68, p < .001, Kendall's W = .23, as did a mixed model of the square root of Percent of time present, F(5, 145) = 9.10, p < .001. Joy time percent was exactly 0 in 113 of the 180 respondent by bin values (62.8%).

**Other expressions.** Surprise peaked in Bin_03 (M = 7.57% of the bin, with Surprise present in 19 of 30 viewers, 63.3%), and the next highest bin was Bin_04 (M = 3.54%); Surprise was at or below 1.49% in the remaining four bins. Positive Valence followed the Joy pattern (M = 11.44% of the commercial, SD = 14.39; Bin_05 M = 24.37% and Bin_06 M = 16.18%), and 28 of 30 viewers had some Positive Valence during the commercial. Brow Furrow was small and flat across the bins (M = 2.37% to 3.86%), and Negative Valence was close to zero (M = 0.45% of the commercial, present in 8 of 30 viewers). Engagement, a raw mean score, rose from M = 7.44 (SD = 3.18) at Baseline to M = 15.25 (SD = 8.29) in the commercial and was highest in Bin_05 (M = 22.73); these Engagement values are descriptive and were not tested.

**Positive Valence and liking (H3).** Positive Valence during the commercial was positively related to how much viewers liked the ad (Ad_liking_1to7: M = 5.17, SD = 1.32, range 2 to 7). The Pearson correlation was r(28) = .45, 95% CI [.11, .70], p = .012, and the Spearman correlation was rho = .41, 95% CI [.05, .68], p = .023 (t approximation, because ratings tie). Each additional point of liking was associated with 4.98 more percentage points of Positive Valence time, 95% CI [1.20, 8.75], and liking accounted for 21% of the variance in Positive Valence time (R squared = .21). Positive Valence time was strongly right skewed (Shapiro Wilk W = 0.763, p < .001), which is why the Spearman result is reported beside the Pearson result.

**Assumption checks.** The differences for H1 departed from normality (W = 0.816, p < .001), as did the mixed model residuals (W = 0.895, p < .001) and the respondent intercepts (W = 0.738, p < .001). Joy time percent was right skewed (skewness 2.19 at Baseline and 2.09 in the commercial), and the residual SD was 2.38 times larger in the bin with the largest spread than in the bin with the smallest, so the F test for the bins should be read together with the Friedman test and the square root model, which gave the same conclusion. (All values simulated.)

## (c) Discussion

In these simulated data, viewers showed more Joy during the commercial than during the Baseline, and the Joy was concentrated at the end: two thirds of the viewers (66.7%) showed Joy in Bin_05, and more than half (53.3%) did in Bin_06, against 23.3% to 30.0% in each of the first four bins. Surprise peaked earlier, in Bin_03 (63.3% of viewers). With a single commercial, the timing of these peaks can be linked to what is on screen only by replaying the video against the bins. Viewers who showed more Positive Valence during the commercial also reported liking the ad more, a moderate association (r = .45) with a wide interval, based on 30 respondents and on a few viewers with a high share of Positive Valence time. Because the facial coding describes what the face displayed and the rating describes what the viewer reported afterward, the two agreeing is evidence that the expression measure carries information about the response, and it is not evidence that the expression caused the liking or the reverse. The threshold analysis shows that the mean Percent of time present depends strongly on the threshold (17.76%, 10.37%, and 4.21% at 25, 50, and 75), whereas the conclusion that Joy was higher during the commercial did not. A report should therefore state the threshold and show at least one other, and should present the proportion of viewers who showed the expression beside the time percent.

Several limitations are typical of facial expression analysis of commercials with a webcam and would apply to a real version of this study. First, every respondent saw the Baseline first and the commercial second, so the Commercial minus Baseline difference also contains time in the chair, settling in, and any expectation of the commercial; the Baseline is a comparison period and not a counterbalanced control, and a stimulus with no emotional content, or a second commercial in a counterbalanced order, would strengthen the comparison. Second, AFFDEX returns a score from a trained classifier for each frame, and a thresholded Joy segment means that the score passed the threshold, not that the viewer felt joy; movement of the mouth for other reasons, such as speaking, can raise the score, and an emotion that the viewer did not show on the face is not recorded. Facial expression coding measures the valence and expressiveness of the face and says nothing about arousal, which a measure such as skin conductance would add. Third, the quality of the recording limits what can be analyzed: the two exclusions here (glare on glasses lenses and a viewer who looked down at a phone) show how lighting, glasses, and posture reduce the share of frames that AFFDEX can analyze, and the Data quality value and the exclusion rule belong in the report. Fourth, the measures are zero inflated and skewed, so means are pulled by a few expressive viewers and a bar chart of means hides the shape; the count of viewers with any expression, the median, and a rank based test should accompany the mean. Fifth, the bins are 10 s long by choice, and a different bin length or a different definition of a viewer showing an expression in a bin (for example, a minimum number of seconds above the threshold) would change the proportions, so the definition should be reported. Sixth, three hypotheses were tested without correction in a sample of 30 respondents who watched one commercial in a laboratory, knowing that a camera recorded them, which can change how freely they express emotion; a replication with more respondents and more than one commercial is needed before any practical recommendation. Finally, these numbers are simulated for teaching and were generated to follow the planned effects described in `make_simulated_data.py`; they demonstrate the analysis, not the effect.
