# Model paragraphs for the data preparation and analysis write up (Workshop 7)

**SIMULATED DATA.** The paragraphs below are models for students. Every statistic in them comes from the SIMULATED export bundle that `make_simulated_data.py` generated (seed 20271231) and from the two output files `prepare_output.txt` (the data preparation pipeline, `prepare_data.R`) and `analysis_output.txt` (the tests, `analysis.R`); numbers are copied from those files and rounded to two decimals (p values to three). No real person was recorded, and the numbers must never be quoted as findings. The paragraphs show the form: how to report how exports were cleaned and merged, with counts at every step, how to report three tests that need merged data in APA style, and how to discuss what data management decisions can and cannot fix.

**Parts of the export layouts are modeled and must be verified on the lab computer before real data are analyzed** (README.md, section 5): among them the R metrics file names and identifier columns, the FEA metric columns, the Sensor Data channel names for the Aurora and the Shimmer PPG, the separators of the data quality and survey files, and the meaning of "Start (ms)" in the R exports.

## (a) Data preparation and methods

**Study and exports.** The study was built in iMotions Lab 11.1 (11.1.47136.14) as a screen based study at the lab desktop with the Smart Eye Aurora (60 Hz), the respondent camera with Affectiva AFFDEX, and a Shimmer3 GSR+ (128 Hz). Twenty-four participants (P01 to P24) saw an instruction slide, a 60 s Baseline, two 30 s video ads for two brands of reusable water bottles (Ad_A, ALTAVO; Ad_B, BRENLOW) in counterbalanced order, and a survey (liking of each ad, 1 to 7; brand recall for each ad, multiple choice; a three item attitude scale with one reverse worded item; purchase intention, 1 to 7). Each ad carried three areas of interest (Logo, Product, Headline) and one stimulus annotation for the end card (25,000 to 30,000 ms after ad onset). After collection, the exports of seven families and the lab session log were saved into one folder each: Sensor Data (one file per recording; P01 to P06 were exported for the raw data checks), the AOI Metrics Export (individual and aggregated files), the FEA Metrics Export (threshold 50), GSR Peak Metrics, the full survey response matrix and the survey statistics, the data quality file, the annotation export (individual and aggregate files), and the lab session log, 18 files in all (including a copy of the survey statistics that a student had opened and saved again in Excel). Three rules were written in the study protocol before collection: use only the recordings that the session log marks for use (R1); exclude a participant from all analyses when eye tracking quality is below 90 percent or the face was found in fewer than 80 percent of frames during either ad (R2); and, for GSR analyses only, treat a stimulus as missing when GSR signal covers less than 80 percent of its duration (R3).

**Cleaning and merging.** All files were read with R 4.3.3 (readr, dplyr, tidyr) as text, from the header row found by rule (the line after "#DATA", or the first line that starts with the expected first column name), without trimming spaces, so that every key could be inspected before it was changed. Two respondent keys did not match the session log character for character: "p07" in the survey matrix (1 row) and "P07 " with a trailing space in the data quality file (5 rows); both had been exported before the name was corrected in iMotions. Keys were standardized (spaces trimmed, upper case) and mapped to participant codes through the session log, which listed 25 recordings for 24 participants: P04 had been recorded twice after a failed calibration, and rule R1 removed the aborted first recording (2 rows of the GSR file, 6 rows of the FEA file, 2 rows of the data quality file, and one Sensor Data file). One participant's details had been left at the iMotions defaults (gender OTHER, age 0, group Default); gender and age were set to missing, and the order of the ads was recovered from the stimulus start times, which agreed with the session log for all 24 participants. The survey matrix was reshaped from 8 pairs of LABELID_ and LABELVALUE_ columns into 192 answers (24 participants by 8 questions); the recall items were scored against the answer key (1 = correct brand, 0 = any other answer), and the reverse worded attitude item was recoded (8 minus the answer). Cronbach's alpha of the attitude scale was .84 after recoding (-1.10 without it; n = 24; attitude M = 4.50, SD = 0.99). Every key and every rule decision was written to a cleaning log (20 entries), and each variable to a codebook (Broman & Woo, 2018; Wickham, 2014; Wilson et al., 2017).

**Consistency checks.** The raw Sensor Data of six participants were compared with the metrics exports. The share of eye tracker samples inside the Logo AOI between the StartMedia and EndMedia events agreed with the exported gaze dwell time in percent (mean absolute difference 0.03 percentage points, largest 0.28; r = .999 over 12 participant by ad pairs), and the share of AFFDEX frames with Joy at or above 50 reproduced the exported percent of time (largest difference 0.00 percentage points). The R metrics clock ran a constant 18,429 to 28,064 ms behind the Sensor Data clock, depending on the participant (spread within a participant at most 0.7 ms), and the annotation export counted from the ad's onset: times were compared only after conversion to one clock. Survey item means reproduced the iMotions survey statistics, and the logo hit rates reproduced the aggregated AOI file.

**Exclusions and the analysis sample.** Rule R2 excluded two participants (P18, eye tracking quality 77 percent during Ad_B; P21, face found in 66 and 65 percent of frames during the ads). The analysis sample was 22 participants (12 women, 9 men, 1 not recorded; age M = 21.33 years, SD = 1.88, n = 21; 11 in each order). Rule R3 set one GSR value to missing (P05, Ad_B: the Shimmer battery failed after 7.8 s, leaving 26.0 percent of the signal); a 5.2 s connection gap in another participant's Baseline left 91.3 percent of the signal and was kept, as the rule specifies. The merged data set has one row per participant and stimulus (72 rows, 22 participants and 66 rows in the analysis sample; 65 rows for GSR analyses) and one row per participant (24 rows).

**Analysis.** All tests were two tailed with alpha = .05. H1 (brand recall by Logo fixation) used Fisher's exact test and its conditional odds ratio on the 2 x 2 table pooled over the two ads; because each participant contributes two observations, the test was repeated for each ad and checked with a mixed effects logistic regression with a random intercept per participant (lme4; Bates et al., 2015). H2 (Joy and liking) used a linear mixed model of liking on the percent of time with Joy (per 10 percentage points) and the ad, with a random intercept per participant and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (lmerTest), and Pearson and Spearman correlations for each ad. H3 (GSR peaks per minute, each ad versus Baseline) used paired t tests with Cohen's d_z and its noncentral t confidence interval (Lakens, 2013), Wilcoxon signed rank tests as checks, and Holm's adjustment over the two ads.

## (b) Results

**H1: Logo fixation and brand recall.** Participants fixated the Logo in 12 of 22 cases for each ad and recalled the advertised brand in 12 of 22 cases for each ad. Pooled over the two ads, the brand was recalled in 17 of 24 observations in which the Logo had been fixated (70.8%) and in 7 of 20 observations in which it had not (35.0%), OR = 4.34, 95% CI [1.08, 19.55], Fisher's exact p = .032; χ²(1, N = 44) = 5.65, p = .017, φ = .36. The association had the same direction for each ad separately, with wide intervals that included 1 (Ad_A: OR = 6.31, 95% CI [0.80, 68.43], p = .084; Ad_B: OR = 2.85, 95% CI [0.39, 24.13], p = .391). A mixed logistic regression with a random intercept per participant gave OR = 4.51, 95% CI [1.26, 16.10], z = 2.32, p = .020; the participant variance was estimated at zero (a singular fit), so the model reduced to an ordinary logistic regression. H1 was supported, with the caution that the pooled observations are not fully independent and the interval is wide.

**H2: Joy and liking.** Participants showed Joy during 12.13 percent of Ad_A (SD = 10.81) and 8.33 percent of Ad_B (SD = 9.46), with no Joy in 11 of the 44 participant by ad observations; mean liking was 4.59 (SD = 1.40) for Ad_A and 4.05 (SD = 1.29) for Ad_B. In the mixed model, liking was 0.32 points higher for every 10 percentage points of Joy, SE = 0.20, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.71], t(40.66) = 1.63, p = .110; the standardized slope was 0.24, and 37 percent of the variance in liking lay between participants (intraclass correlation = .37). The correlations for each ad were small to moderate and not significant (Ad_A: r(20) = .27, 95% CI [-.18, .62], p = .232, Spearman ρ = .33; Ad_B: r(20) = .18, 95% CI [-.26, .56], p = .424, Spearman ρ = .14). H2 was not supported at alpha = .05; the estimate was positive, but a sample of 22 participants cannot distinguish a small positive relation from none.

**H3: GSR peaks per minute.** GSR peaks were more frequent during Ad_A (M = 7.27 peaks per minute, SD = 6.80) than during Baseline (M = 2.06, SD = 1.86), mean difference = 5.21, 95% CI [2.41, 8.01], t(21) = 3.87, p < .001, d_z = 0.83, 95% CI [0.33, 1.30]; 19 of 22 participants increased. Among the 21 participants with valid GSR during Ad_B, peaks were more frequent during Ad_B (M = 6.00, SD = 4.94) than during Baseline (M = 1.88, SD = 1.68), mean difference = 4.12, 95% CI [2.15, 6.10], t(20) = 4.35, p < .001, d_z = 0.95, 95% CI [0.42, 1.46]; 16 of 21 increased. Both effects remained significant after Holm's adjustment (both adjusted p < .001). The differences for Ad_A were skewed by a few strong responders (Shapiro Wilk W = 0.862, p = .006), and the Wilcoxon signed rank tests agreed with the t tests (Ad_A: V = 212, p < .001; Ad_B: V = 165, p < .001). H3 was supported for both ads.

## (c) Discussion: what the data preparation taught, and its limits

**Data management lessons.** None of the three tests was possible with one export alone: H1 joined the AOI export to the survey, H2 the FEA export to the survey, and H3 needed the data quality file and the session log to decide which GSR values to trust. The joins failed first on things that no statistics course mentions. A respondent name typed with a trailing space, or retyped in lower case, survived in every file exported before the correction; Excel lookups ignore case and would have hidden one of the two problems, while a join on raw keys followed by a quality filter would have dropped two participants without a warning (Kovacs et al., 2021). A participant recorded twice could be resolved only because the operator wrote down which recording to use, which makes the session log the key table of the study. The exports also counted time from three different zero points (the recording start, the study start, and the stimulus onset) and placed tables below blocks of metadata whose length changes between releases; reading files by rule rather than by row number, and checking raw samples against the exported metrics, caught both before analysis. Files were never edited by hand: every change is a line of code and a line in the cleaning log, so the whole path from exports to results can be repeated (Wilson et al., 2017; Broman & Woo, 2018), and the codebook states for each variable what a missing value means (Ellis & Leek, 2018). A copy that a student had opened and saved again in Excel used another separator and decimal mark; it held the same numbers, but spreadsheet programs can also turn codes into dates without a warning (Ziemann et al., 2016), so analysis read only the raw exports.

**Rules written in advance.** The exclusion rule for data quality, the GSR signal rule, the recall answer key, and the scoring of the attitude scale were fixed in the protocol before the data were seen, which removes the temptation to choose exclusions after looking at the results (Simmons et al., 2011). The rules also separate two kinds of loss: a participant whose eye tracking or face data failed was excluded from every analysis, whereas a participant whose GSR failed during one ad lost only that value, for the GSR test only, rather than the whole case (Schafer & Graham, 2002). No outlier rule was written in advance, so the strong GSR responders stayed in the data and the rank based Wilcoxon test served as the check; a future protocol could name a robust rule, such as the median absolute deviation (Leys et al., 2013), before collection.

**Limitations.** The data are simulated, and several export layouts are modeled rather than documented; the pipeline must be run on a real export from the lab computer, and its column names corrected, before it is trusted. The sample is small: H2's interval runs from a slightly negative to a moderate slope, and the per ad tests of H1 have intervals too wide to interpret alone. The pooled H1 test treats two observations from the same person as independent; the mixed model is the better guide, and its singular fit says only that this sample showed no extra similarity within participants. The raw data checks covered six of 24 participants. One participant's age and gender are missing because the details were never entered. Finally, two ads cannot represent ads in general: with ads as a fixed factor, the results describe these two ads only (Judd et al., 2012).
