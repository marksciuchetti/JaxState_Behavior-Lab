Workshop 1 of 9

From question to publication with the Neon glasses: an in-store shelf study

This workshop follows one marketing question from the first sentence a client says to the last sentence of a written results and discussion section. By the end you will be able to design a shelf study, build it in iMotions Lab 11.1, run a field day with the Neon glasses, map the recordings onto one photograph of the shelf, measure attention with areas of interest, test a hypothesis, and write the result in a form a journal or a client would accept. Every section explains why a step exists before it shows how.

Mobile eye tracking records where a person's eyes point while that person moves through a real place, and for how long. Eye tracking glasses carry small infrared eye cameras and a forward facing scene camera, so the recording shows the world as the wearer saw it with a gaze marker drawn on top. That is what the method is. It is not a window into reasons or feelings: eye movements tell you what was looked at, in what order, and for how long, not why or whether the wearer liked it (Wedel & Pieters, 2008). Attention predicts choice; it does not measure it (Orquin & Mueller Loose, 2013).

A shelf is the right first study because so much marketing is decided there. A large share of brand decisions is made in the store, and the number and position of a brand's facings change how much attention it gets and how it is evaluated at the point of purchase (Chandon, Hutchinson, Bradlow, & Young, 2009). In a real supermarket, shelf position and visual salience predict both looking and buying (Gidlöf, Anikin, Lingonblad, & Wallin, 2017), and early in-store glasses work read the sequence of fixations at the shelf as a record of the buying decision (Clement, 2007). The worked example is the iMotions In-Store Shopper sample report: fifteen shoppers in Copenhagen shopped an oral care shelf with and without new point of sale material (POSM) for Sensodyne while wearing eye tracking glasses, then answered a survey.

The equipment is the lab's own. The Pupil Labs Neon glasses record through the Neon Companion app on the Android phone in the case, and the recordings are imported into iMotions Lab 11.1 on the lab laptop, whose license key (0001) is the only one in the lab with the Eye Tracking Glasses module. The Smart Eye Aurora bar is the screen based tracker for images, videos, and websites and is not used here; the Shimmer units, the Enobio EEG, and the webcam platform belong to later workshops.

The one sentence this workshop keeps repeating

Decide what you will compare, and how you will measure it, before anyone puts the glasses on. A glasses recording produces hundreds of numbers for every shopper, so a study without a comparison written in advance will always find something, and that something may be chance.

01

From idea to research question

A client rarely arrives with a research question. A client arrives with a business question, a statement of what the business wants to happen: more shoppers should notice our brand, the new display should earn its cost. The iMotions design guide asks you to begin there and to dig below the brief rather than take its wording at face value, because the business question decides what is worth measuring. Sensodyne's brief in the sample report is the example: the brand had created new point of sale material for the shelf and wanted to understand how it changed the shopper's experience at the shelf, and how that experience differed when the material was absent. That names a wish and a comparison, but not who will be observed, what will be measured, or what would count as a difference.

A research question identifies the main problem you aim to answer and signals the type of study you need. A hypothesis is a testable answer to that question, and it includes the specific variables that will be tested. The guide is explicit about the order: hypotheses are generated before the study is designed and before any data are collected. Writing the expected answer down first forces you to say what you would accept as evidence, and it protects you from confirmation bias, the tendency to see what you expected and to explain away what you did not. The guide allows exploratory studies in commercial settings, with the same warning attached.

The lab asks every study to pass through one sentence before anything is built. The sentence has four parts: who (the population), sees what (the stimuli and conditions), measured how (the sensor and the metric), and compared against what (the other condition, a baseline, or a benchmark). For the shelf study the sentence reads: regular buyers of dental care products (who) shop an oral care shelf either with or without the Sensodyne wing point of sale material (sees what) while the glasses record where they look, from which we compute how soon and how long they look at Sensodyne products (measured how), and we compare the two shelf layouts (against what). If you cannot fill all four slots, you do not yet have a study.

The variables are the objects that will be manipulated and measured. What you change is the independent variable; what you measure in response is the dependent variable. In the shelf study the independent variable is the shelf layout, with two levels, POSM and No POSM. The dependent variables are the attention metrics: the time to first fixation (TTFF) on Sensodyne products, which is how many seconds pass from the moment the shopper reaches the shelf until the first fixation lands inside the Sensodyne area of interest (AOI), a named region drawn on the shelf image; the dwell time, the total time spent looking inside that region; and the respondent ratio, the percentage of shoppers who looked at the region at all. The guide adds a distinction that matters later for the statistics: a variable is discrete when it can only be counted (how many shoppers noticed the wing) and continuous when it can take any value on a scale (seconds until the first fixation). TTFF and dwell time are continuous; the respondent ratio comes from a yes or no count.

Most weak questions fail in one of three ways, and each can be repaired step by step. The first is an opinion: "Do shoppers like our new shelf display?" Liking is not something the glasses measure, so the repair replaces the feeling with an observable, "Do shoppers notice Sensodyne products sooner when the wing is on the shelf?", and then adds the measure and the comparison: "Is TTFF on Sensodyne products shorter at the POSM shelf than at the No POSM shelf among regular dental care buyers?" The second is too general: "What do people look at in the store?" Everything is the answer, so nothing is testable. The repair narrows the place and the regions: "Which of six named shelf regions receives the largest share of fixation time, and does that share change when the wing is present?" The third is missing its what and how: "Does POSM work?" The repair names the mechanism and the metric: "Does the wing raise the percentage of shoppers who fixate Sensodyne products, and shorten the time until they do?" The iMotions table below gives the same contrast in general form: good questions are specific, measurable, and name their variables; bad questions are general, opinion based, or lack a what or a how.

A study run "just to see what we can see" will see something

The iMotions guide warns against fishing expeditions in its own words: biometric measures are granular enough that you can almost always find some result, but without a specific question that result may be due to random chance. A glasses recording yields dozens of metrics for every region of every shelf for every shopper. If you test all of them and report the ones that came out significant, you will publish noise.

The warning has a research literature behind it. Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn showed that ordinary, defensible choices made after looking at the data (which measure to report, when to stop collecting, which participants to drop, which covariates to add) can raise the false positive rate of a study from the nominal five percent to well above fifty (Simmons, Nelson, & Simonsohn, 2011). The remedy is to make those choices before the data exist and to write them down where they cannot quietly change. That is the idea behind preregistration, a time stamped record of the hypotheses, the sample size, the exclusion rules, and the analysis plan, filed before data collection, so that a reader can tell a prediction from a story told afterward (Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, & Mellor, 2018). For a class project the record can be a dated document in the study folder; for a publication it can be a public registry. Either way, section 02 will ask you to write the sample size, the exclusion rule, and the AOI list before the first shopper, and section 08 will hold you to them.

You also need to know what attention metrics can and cannot answer before you promise anything. They answer where (which region), when (how soon), how long (dwell), how often (revisits), and in what order. They do not answer why. A long dwell on a price tag can mean interest, confusion, or surprise at the number, and the recording looks the same in all three cases. Reading a mental state directly off a physiological signal is called reverse inference, and consumer neuroscience reviews warn that it is the easiest mistake in the field (Plassmann, Venkatraman, Huettel, & Yoon, 2015). That is why the sample study, and yours, pair the glasses with a short survey: the glasses say what was looked at, and the shopper says what she remembers, intends, and chose.

Now the worked example. The sample report lists three questions on its second page. Question 1 asks how the presence of POSM directs shoppers' visual attention at the shelf. Question 2 asks whether the presence of POSM helps drive notice of Sensodyne products. Question 3 asks how shoppers visually consume the POSM itself while at the shelf. Each becomes a hypothesis once the variables are named. H1, from question 1: shoppers at the POSM shelf fixate Sensodyne products before competitor products more often, and spend a smaller share of their shelf time on competitor products, than shoppers at the No POSM shelf. H2, from question 2: time to first fixation on Sensodyne products is shorter at the POSM shelf, and the percentage of shoppers who fixate them is at least as high. H3, from question 3, which concerns the POSM group only: most shoppers who see the wing fixate all three of its elements (the product image, the main message, and the sub message), and the product image receives the longest looking time. H3 is descriptive rather than a comparison between groups. That is allowed, as long as it is written in advance and reported as a description, not dressed up afterward as a test.

Each hypothesis commits to a direction and a metric: H1 an order and a share, H2 a time and a ratio, H3 a pattern across three regions. Prior work gives reasons for the directions: in-store signage attracts attention at the point of purchase (Otterbring, Wästlund, Gustafsson, & Shams, 2014), and merchandise displays draw and hold the eye in ways that ordinary shelf stock does not (Huddleston, Behe, Minahan, & Fernandez, 2015). A hypothesis grounded in a reason is also one you can learn from when it fails.

02

Turning the question into a study

A design is the set of decisions that make a comparison fair: who sees which condition, what is held constant, what the shopper is told, how many shoppers there are, and what is written down before the first one arrives. The first decision is how to allocate people to conditions. In a between subjects design each participant is assigned to only one level of the independent variable, so one group sees the POSM shelf and a different group sees the No POSM shelf. In a within subjects design every participant experiences all conditions. The iMotions design guide lays out the trade: between subjects sessions are shorter but need more participants; within subjects studies cost less and have less error from personality and mood, because each person serves as his or her own control, but participants tire, and the order of conditions can itself change the results.

For a real shelf the choice is nearly forced. The wing cannot be removed while the shopper stands there without telling her what the study is about, and a second visit later the same day does not help, because by then she knows where Sensodyne sits and the first visit has changed the second. That carryover is what a within subjects design must avoid, and here it cannot. The sample report lists fifteen shoppers and two shelf layouts without stating how they were allocated; this workshop treats the study as between subjects, and the simulated data set in section 03 is built that way, with fifteen shoppers per shelf. Within subjects becomes possible when the two versions are physically separate, such as two end caps in different aisles or a lab shelf restocked while the participant waits behind a screen. Then the order is balanced: half see the POSM version first and half second, so that practice and fatigue cancel out when the groups are combined. The guide calls this counterbalancing, and the order each participant received must then be recorded as a variable.

The second decision is what to hold constant. A comparison between two shelves is a comparison of the wing only if everything else is the same: the same shelf in the same aisle, the same lighting and time of day band, the same stock and facings (the planogram, the diagram of what sits where), the same price tags, the same operator script. If the POSM shelf also happened to be fuller, or better lit, or visited in the morning while the other was visited at closing time, the measured difference belongs to a mixture of causes that no statistic can separate later. The iMotions design guide illustrates the rule with a question about music in a creative: the good design holds the visual constant across six music versions, the less optimal design varies several factors at once. Change one thing.

The third decision is the task instruction, and it matters more than beginners expect. The sample report tells the shoppers to explore the oral care shelf as if they were naturally shopping for oral care products, and then to purchase a product. Every word is doing work. "As you normally would" asks for the ordinary browsing the client cares about; a search instruction ("find the Sensodyne") would force attention to the brand and erase the comparison, while a free look with no goal produces wandering that resembles no real shopping trip. "Then choose one product" gives a behavioral outcome, the product chosen, that can be paired with the attention data. The instruction must not name the brand, the wing, or the purpose, because a participant who guesses what the researcher hopes to see tends to supply it. That distortion is a demand effect, and the design guide's rule on consent, to inform participants without priming them with critical information about the expected outcome, applies to the instruction as well. Prepare the instruction in writing in advance, read it the same way to every shopper, and keep it to the sentence above.

The fourth decision is how many shoppers. The honest answer depends on two ideas. The effect size is how far apart the two group means are, measured in units of the spread within the groups; the iMotions guide defines it as a value that quantifies the magnitude of the difference between two group averages. The common version is Cohen's d: the difference between the means divided by the standard deviation. A d of 0.2 is conventionally small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 large (Cohen, 1992). Statistical power is the probability that your study will detect an effect of a given size if that effect is real; the usual target is 80 percent. Power rises with the sample size and with the effect size, and falls as the data get noisier. A study with low power that finds nothing has learned almost nothing, and a study with low power that finds something tends to overstate how large the effect is.

In plain words

Suppose the wing really does shorten the time to first fixation on Sensodyne by a large amount, about four fifths of a standard deviation. How many shoppers per shelf would give you an 80 percent chance of seeing that difference as significant at the usual threshold? Twenty six per shelf, fifty two in all. If the true effect is only medium, the number more than doubles to sixty four per shelf. Fifteen in total, split across two shelves, has power to find only very large effects, so the sample study is best read as a commercial pilot that shows the method and the direction, not as a confirmatory test.

The same calculation as G*Power asks for it
Test family: t tests. Test: means, difference between two independent groups. Tails: two. Effect size d = 0.8. Alpha = .05. Power = .80. Allocation ratio 1. Output: n = 26 per group, total N = 52.

G*Power is the free program most researchers use for this a priori calculation, and it is cited in the method as software (Faul, Erdfelder, Lang, & Buchner, 2007). Cohen's power primer tabulates the same answers: 26 per group for a large effect and 64 for a medium one at this alpha and power (Cohen, 1992). When you report the result, give d with its confidence interval, because a sample this small estimates the effect imprecisely even when the test is significant (Lakens, 2013). If the analysis will model several AOIs per shopper at once, the number of regions per person also matters, and a mixed model tutorial shows how to plan for it (Brysbaert & Stevens, 2018).

Two practical rules follow. First, the iMotions guide recommends 20 to 60 people in each study condition for adequate power, and the lab's own planning convention for a glasses study is 20 to 30 usable recordings per condition. Second, recruit 20 to 25 percent more than the usable target, because glasses studies lose recordings to a poor fit, bad lighting, a shopper who turns away from the shelf, or a phone that stops; 26 usable per shelf means scheduling about 32. Decide the exclusion rule now as well: the simulated study in section 03 drops any recording with less than 70 percent valid gaze data, and that threshold was written before collection, which is the only time it can be written without bias.

The fifth decision is what to record about each shopper. A respondent variable is a fact about the participant stored with the recording so that it can be used later to compare or filter: an anonymous ID (P01, P02, never a name), the condition (POSM or NoPOSM), the order if the design is within subjects, an age band, gender, whether the person regularly buys the category (the sample report screened for regular purchasers of dental products), whether the person wears corrective lenses, how familiar the person is with the store (familiarity changes how shoppers navigate (Otterbring, Wästlund, & Gustafsson, 2016)), the session date, and the operator. iMotions stores name, age, gender, and group with each respondent, and section 04 shows where; everything else goes in the session log.

The sixth decision is the AOI master list, written before collection and used unchanged for both shelves. An area of interest is a measurement decision: the metrics exist only for the regions you draw, so the regions must come from the hypotheses. For the shelf study the list is Sensodyne_Products, Sensodyne_POSM_Wing (POSM shelf only), Competitor_A, Competitor_B, Price_Tags, and Shelf_Other, with the same names and the same boundaries on both layouts. Draw each region a little larger than the object, because no tracker is perfect and a fixation that lands just outside a tight boundary is lost; the size of that margin should follow the tracker's accuracy, and small elements such as the wing's text lines are the ones most at risk (Orquin, Ashby, & Clarke, 2016). The iMotions AOI article says the same thing in one line: decide the AOIs and the metrics before data collection.

The seventh decision is the reference image, one still photograph of each shelf layout onto which every shopper's gaze will later be projected. That projection is called gaze mapping, and sections 06 and 07 explain it; what matters now is that it works only if the photograph is good. The iMotions guidance is that the image should be well lit, sharp, not warped, with clear contrast, and should show the objects as they appear in the recording. Take it straight on, in the store's own light, before the first shopper, and take it again if a single facing moves. Consent is not a formality, and a store is a public place

The scene camera records whatever the shopper faces, which in a store includes other shoppers and staff who did not agree to anything. The iMotions glasses guide says plainly that an in-store study may need explicit permission from the store and that other people may not want to be recorded. Get the store's permission in writing, tell the wearer that bystanders will appear in the video, and plan to blur faces before any recording leaves the lab (section 05 shows the button).

What the shopper agrees to is informed consent: a documented explanation, given before participation, of the purpose of the study, that participation is voluntary and can be withdrawn at any time, the participant's rights, how the data will be handled and stored, and the possible risks. The iMotions consent FAQ lists exactly those five items and recommends sharing the study information at recruitment and having the form signed on the day. The design guide adds that consent must inform without priming: say that the study is about shopping and attention, not that it is about the wing. Gaze and scene video are more revealing than they look, since what a person looks at can indicate interests, health, and identity, which is why the recordings deserve the same care as medical data (Kröger, Lutz, & Müller, 2020). Consumer neuroscience ethics turns on the same points: participants must understand what is recorded, results must not be used to manipulate vulnerable people, and claims must stay within what the method supports (Stanton, Sinnott-Armstrong, & Huettel, 2017). The Neuromarketing Science and Business Association code of ethics sets out the consent, privacy, and participant rights articles that commercial studies are expected to follow (Neuromarketing Science & Business Association, n.d.).

At Jacksonville State every study that will be published or presented goes through the Institutional Review Board (IRB), the university committee that reviews research with human participants before it begins. Use the lab's consent template, submit the protocol with the instruction script and the survey, and when the approval letter arrives file it, with the protocol number, in the study's 00_Admin folder, because the methods section and the journal will ask for it. Finally, run a pilot: two people end to end, from consent through export, before you recruit anyone, so that the measures you promised come out of the software the way you planned.

03

The worked example in full

Everything from sections 01 and 02 fits on one page, and writing that page is the first deliverable of any study in this lab. Below is the Sensodyne shelf study written as a student study plan. Where the sample report gives a fact, the plan uses it and says so; where the report is silent (the allocation of shoppers to shelves, the exclusion rule, the statistical tests), the plan fills the gap with the decisions a student would have to make, and the simulated data set follows those decisions. Read it as a template: replace the brand, the regions, and the numbers, and you have the plan for your own study.

Question
Does new point of sale material for Sensodyne change where shoppers look at an oral care shelf, how soon they notice Sensodyne products, and how they look at the material itself? (The report's three questions, page 2.)
Hypotheses
H1: at the POSM shelf, shoppers fixate Sensodyne products before competitor products more often and spend a smaller share of shelf time on competitors. H2: time to first fixation on Sensodyne products is shorter at the POSM shelf and the respondent ratio is at least as high. H3 (POSM group only, descriptive): most shoppers fixate all three wing elements, and the product image receives the longest looking time.
Design
Between subjects, one factor (shelf layout) with two levels. Each shopper visits one shelf once. Random assignment to shelf by a list prepared before the field day.
Conditions and controls
POSM: the shelf with the Sensodyne wing mounted. No POSM: the same shelf without it. Same aisle, same stock and facings, same price tags, same lighting and time of day band, same operator script.
Stimuli
Two shelf layouts (the report's "Stimuli Tested: Two Shelf Layouts"). One reference photograph of each layout, taken straight on in store light before the first shopper.
AOIs
Sensodyne_Products, Sensodyne_POSM_Wing (POSM layout only, with three child regions: product image, main message, sub message), Competitor_A, Competitor_B, Price_Tags, Shelf_Other. Drawn on the reference images with a margin; the list is final before collection.
Sample
The report: N = 15, ages 18 to 59, 8 men and 7 women, regular purchasers of dental products, Copenhagen, March 2020. The simulated version: 30 shoppers, 15 per shelf, so that the statistics of section 08 have something to work with; a confirmatory study would plan 26 per shelf and recruit 32.
Task
Explore the oral care shelf as you normally would when shopping for oral care products, then purchase a product (the report's instruction). Glasses off after the purchase; questions answered afterward; then the survey.
Measures
Eye tracking: time to first fixation on each AOI, time spent (dwell time) in each AOI, percent of shoppers who fixated each AOI (respondent ratio), and the navigation order in which AOIs were first fixated. Survey: purchase intent for Sensodyne on a 1 to 7 scale, unaided brand recall, and the product chosen.
Analysis plan
Exclude recordings with less than 70 percent valid gaze data. Primary test: Welch's t test on TTFF for Sensodyne_Products between shelves, with Cohen's d and its 95 percent confidence interval. Secondary: dwell share on Sensodyne_Products; combined competitor dwell; a mixed model of dwell share across AOIs with a random intercept per shopper. Survey: t test for intent, exact tests for recall and choice. All two tailed at alpha = .05.
Deliverables
Heat maps and navigation paths per shelf, an AOI table by condition, three figures, a results section and a discussion in journal form, and a client executive summary.

The measures deserve a closer look, because the report and the software use different words for the same things. The report's deliverables page defines three AOI numbers: Looking Time, Time to Notice, and Percent to Notice, plus heat maps and numbered navigation paths. In iMotions the same quantities appear under the names of the AOI Metrics article: Looking Time is dwell time, the total time the gaze or the fixations spent inside the region; Time to Notice is TTFF, the time from the start of the interval until the first fixation inside the region, where a fixation is a period of a few hundred milliseconds during which the eye rests on one spot; and Percent to Notice is the respondent ratio. The standard handbook of eye tracking measures gives the formal definitions and the alternatives, and it is the book to cite when a reviewer asks what "dwell" means (Holmqvist et al., 2011). A heat map is a color overlay showing where attention concentrated across all shoppers, red for most and transparent for none; it is a picture, not a number, and the report uses it to show, not to test. A navigation path is the numbered order in which regions were first looked at, averaged over shoppers; the report's path figures are what H1 is about.

Each hypothesis has its measure, and the pairing is the heart of the plan. H1 is tested by the navigation order (which region came first) and by the share of dwell time on competitors. H2 is tested by TTFF on Sensodyne_Products, the primary outcome, with the respondent ratio as a check that the shorter time is not an artifact of fewer people looking. H3 is described by the respondent ratio and the dwell time for each of the three wing regions in the POSM group. The survey adds purchase intent and recall, which no gaze metric can supply. One more measurement decision belongs here: the shelf interval. A shopper's recording starts before she reaches the shelf and ends after she leaves it, so every metric must be computed over the segment from arrival at the shelf to the moment the chosen product is in hand. An in-store glasses study of decision making describes shelf visits as an orientation phase, an evaluation phase, and a verification phase, which is a useful way to decide where the segment starts and ends (Gidlöf, Wallin, Dewhurst, & Holmqvist, 2013). In iMotions that segmentation is done with the gaze mapping interval and annotations, in section 07.

Why keep the survey if the glasses already show what shoppers looked at? Because the two measure different things, and the client's question needs both. Physiological and behavioral measures capture the response in the moment, before the shopper has had time to rationalize it; self report captures what the shopper remembers, believes, and intends after the fact, and lets her say why. Reviews of consumer neuroscience methods recommend exactly this pairing rather than either alone, because each corrects a weakness of the other (Bell et al., 2018), and plain language overviews written for marketing researchers say the same (Harris, Ciorciari, & Gountas, 2018). The sample report's self report page makes the practical case: in shopper testing the survey covers intent to purchase, likeability, and recall, and some researchers also watch the live signals during the visit and then probe the shopper with specific questions afterward. H2 can be true and purchase intent can still be flat; without the survey you would never know.

The numbers in sections 06 and 08 to 10 are simulated

The sample report gives a handful of figures: time spent on competitor products falls from 127.9 seconds without POSM to 94.3 seconds with it, almost 25 percent less; all shoppers explored every element of the wing; the product image was noticed first, after 28 seconds in the context of the whole shelf, and received 1.1 seconds of looking time against 0.3 for the main message and 0.2 for the sub message. It does not publish per shopper data, standard deviations, or tests. So the workshop uses a SIMULATED data set built to mirror the report's design and the direction of its findings. Every number from that set is labeled simulated, and none may ever be quoted as a finding about real shoppers.

The simulated set has three files in the site's data folder, all generated by one script (make_simulated_data.py, seed 20261037) and all carrying a first line that says so. The field day

A field day is a sequence of small habits, and the recordings you get back are only as good as the weakest one. The order matters, so this section runs through the day in order: what is done before the first shopper, what happens with each shopper, how the files leave the phone, and how they arrive in iMotions. The iMotions glasses guide's first instruction is the one to remember: before inviting any participant, pilot the whole protocol on yourself or a colleague, from fitting the glasses to exporting the data and answering the question, because that is the only way to find out where the plan breaks. A published field protocol for glasses in a real store follows the same sequence of consent, fitting, a gaze check, a natural shopping task, and a short survey (Wästlund, Otterbring, Gustafsson, & Shams, 2015).

Before the first shopper, four things are in place. The store's written permission and the IRB approval are in the folder. The reference photographs of both shelf layouts have been taken, straight on, sharp, in the store's own light, with the stock exactly as the shoppers will see it (section 02). The phone is charged, the Companion app is open with a wearer created, and the app's Compute eye state and Compute fixations settings are enabled, since the help center lists pupil, blink, and fixation signals as available only when those settings are on. And the session log sheet is printed, one row per shopper, with columns for ID, date, operator, condition, start time, the gaze check result and the number of attempts, the elapsed time at the shelf start and end, incidents, whether the data are complete, and a decision note. The simulated session log in the data folder shows the layout.

With each shopper the sequence is consent, fit, check, instruct, record, debrief, survey, log. Consent first, with the form from section 02, read and signed before the glasses come out. Then the fit. Seat the shopper, choose the nose piece that puts the eye cameras in a clear view of the eyes, settle the frame straight, and fasten the USB-C cable so that a tug on it cannot move the frame; the guide also screens out participants with eye surgery, bifocals, infrared blocking contact lenses, or strong eye makeup, because each degrades the eye image. The Neon is designed to track without a calibration procedure, so there is no sequence of dots to follow (Pupil Labs, n.d.). Do the check anyway. The glasses guide's practical tip is to watch the live view on the recording unit while the wearer holds her head still and looks at known targets, one at the center of her view and others toward the edges, so that accuracy is judged across the field of view and not only straight ahead. The lab's version is five targets: a price tag at the center of the shelf, then one at each of the four corners. If the gaze circle sits beside the target rather than on it, refit and look again, and write the number of attempts in the log.

Why be so careful when the device needs no calibration? Because the quality of glasses data depends on the fit staying put. When a head worn tracker slips, even by a few millimeters, the measured gaze shifts, and the error grows with the slip; that is the central finding of a study of slippage across several head worn trackers, and it is why a frame that is bumped mid session must be checked again (Niehorster et al., 2020). Changes in pupil size also move the apparent gaze position in head mounted trackers; a recent comparison that included the Neon found the smallest such shift for the Neon, which is reassuring but not a reason to skip the check (Salari, Niehorster, Nyström, & Bednarik, 2026). The accuracy and precision figures you will quote in a methods section come from the vendor's own test report, which is a white paper rather than a peer reviewed study and should be cited as one (Baumann & Dierkes, 2023); those figures assume a frame that fits.

Lighting is the next habit. The guide asks you to avoid direct sunlight on the recording, to blind windows if you must, and to remember that the scene camera's automatic gain adjusts brightness to the whole scene, so a sudden bright patch can darken the shelf until it is unreadable in replay. Gaze mapping, the step that projects gaze onto the reference photograph, relies on contrast between the photograph and the video, so the shelf must look the same in both; test that in the pilot at the planned time of day. Then the instruction, read from the card in the same words to every shopper, and the phone into a pocket or onto a belt so that the hands are free and the unit cannot be dropped.

Start the recording in the app and let the shopper go. An import study has no live markers, because iMotions is not running, so the operator supplies spoken markers: a short phrase said aloud at a defined moment, "shelf start" when the shopper arrives at the shelf and "shelf end" when the chosen product is in hand. The glasses guide notes that a recording contains video and sound, so the phrase can be found again in replay and turned into an annotation that marks the shelf interval; whatever the audio setting, also write the elapsed time from the app's counter next to each marker in the log, so that the interval can be set even if the sound is unusable. Walk behind, out of the shopper's line of sight, and watch for the frame sliding; the guide names sliding as the thing to keep an eye on during a recording. After the purchase, stop the recording, remove the glasses, debrief, and give the survey. Then, before the next shopper, review the recording on the phone at increased speed, as the guide recommends, to confirm that the gaze marker sat on the objects the shopper handled; a slipped frame shows as a marker that is consistently beside things rather than on them. Note the result and the battery state in the log.

What goes wrong, and how it looks later. What the data are, and how to describe them so a reader can trust the numbers

Every number that leaves this workshop, a time to first fixation of 2.25 seconds or a dwell share of 18 percent, is the last link in a chain of measurements and decisions: eye cameras produce gaze samples, a filter sorts those samples into fixations and saccades, gaze mapping projects them onto a photograph of the shelf, areas of interest cut the photograph into regions, and metrics summarize what landed in each region. A methods section exists so that a reader can follow that chain and judge whether the numbers mean what you say they mean. The reporting standards for quantitative research in psychology ask for exactly this kind of description of apparatus, processing, and exclusion rules (Appelbaum et al., 2018), and the eye tracking handbook that iMotions itself points to supplies the vocabulary (Holmqvist et al., 2011). This section walks the chain from the first link to the last, then shows what a methods paragraph built from it looks like.

What the glasses record

The Neon glasses carry two inward facing eye cameras, a forward facing scene camera, and an IMU, an inertial measurement unit that records head motion (Pupil Labs, n.d.). From the eye cameras the system computes a gaze sample, one estimate of where the wearer is looking, 200 times per second: the iMotions Neon article states that both import routes deliver the scene camera recording with 200 Hz gaze data, and the Recorded Data card on the lab laptop shows the same figure, 200Hz, next to the gaze sample quality percentage. That rate is the sampling rate. The scene camera runs much slower than the gaze stream; the iMotions glasses guide notes that most scene cameras update at about 30 frames per second, so one second of recording holds roughly 200 gaze points drawn over about 30 video frames. Verify the exact scene camera frame rate of the lab's Neon on the lab machine before you quote it. Accuracy and precision figures for the Neon come from the vendor's own accuracy test report (Baumann & Dierkes, 2023); quote them as vendor figures, not as independent findings. A peer reviewed evaluation that included the Neon found that pupil size changes shift apparent gaze in head mounted trackers, with the smallest shift for the Neon (Salari et al., 2026), which is one reason the fit check in section 05 matters.

Fixations and saccades: define them, do not assume them

A fixation is a period during which the gaze rests on one location long enough for the brain to take in detail; a saccade is the fast jump between two fixations, during which little is seen. The figure below shows the idea. The complication is that the research community does not agree on one definition: a survey of 124 eye movement researchers found substantial disagreement about what counts as a fixation (Hessels et al., 2018). The practical answer is an operational definition: in your methods, a fixation is whatever the filter you name, with the parameters you list, classified as a fixation. Algorithms fall into two families, those that threshold velocity and those that threshold dispersion (Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000); iMotions offers both, and the lab study uses the velocity family.

Glasses add a second complication. When a shopper turns to scan the shelf, the eyes and the head move together, and the gaze point in the scene video can stay on a product while the whole picture slides. The iMotions glasses guide calls fixation analysis on glasses data "heavily debated" for this reason, and the Areas of Interest article says that gaze based metrics "can be preferred" for mobile recordings because head movements and reflex eye movements can disturb the classification. Head motion also lets the frame slip on the nose, and slippage measurably degrades data quality in head worn trackers (Niehorster et al., 2020). The lab's rule is to report fixation based metrics, which the sample report and most published shelf studies use, and to check them against the gaze based twins (gaze based dwell time and hit time) before drawing a conclusion.

The I-VT filter and its parameters

The lab's fixation filter is I-VT, velocity threshold identification. It computes how fast the gaze is moving at each sample, in degrees of visual angle per second, and calls the sample part of a saccade when the speed is at or above a threshold and part of a fixation when it is below. The visual angle idea is shown below: the same shift of a few pixels is a small angle for a viewer far from the display and a large angle for a viewer close to it, so velocities are expressed as angles rather than pixels. For screen based trackers the distance comes from the tracker; for glasses, the I-VT article explains, iMotions uses fixed field of view parameters (width, height, and distance) that newer versions fill in automatically from the manufacturer. Verify on the lab machine, under the study's STUDY SETTINGS tab, that those values are populated for the Neon.

The parameters live on the study's GAZE ANALYSIS tab (Library > click the study > GAZE ANALYSIS). On the lab laptop they read: Fixation Filter I-VT (fixations and saccades); Window Length 20 ms; Velocity Threshold 30 degrees/second; Gap Fill-in (interpolation) checked with Max Gap Length 75 ms; Noise Reduction unchecked; Discard Short Fixations checked with Minimum Fixation Duration 60 ms; Merge Adjacent Fixations checked with Max Time Between Fixations 75 ms and Max Angle Between Fixations 0.5 degrees. The I-VT article explains what each one does. Window Length is the time window around a sample over which velocity is computed; a smaller window reacts faster to changes in velocity. Velocity Threshold is the cutoff between fixation and saccade; raise it and more samples fall below it, so fixations grow and saccades shrink. Gap fill-in interpolates missing data in gaps no longer than the maximum; longer gaps become an unknown eye movement and break fixations. Noise Reduction smooths the coordinates, and on a low sampling tracker it "risks to filter out the signal." Discard Short Fixations reclassifies fixations below the minimum duration as unknown. Merge Adjacent Fixations joins fixations that are close in time or angle into one longer fixation. The article gives no default numbers, so the values above are the lab laptop's current settings, not a vendor recommendation.

One filter, one set of parameters, for every respondent

The classifier runs for the whole study, and every fixation based AOI metric depends on it. Never change the filter or a parameter between conditions or between respondents, and record the values in the project log before the first export. If you change a value later, every metric must be recomputed and the methods paragraph rewritten.

Gaze mapping is a measurement, not a picture

Gaze mapping projects gaze from the moving scene video onto one still photograph of the shelf, the reference image. The help center describes the standard "full-image" algorithm as an object recognition routine that searches for the visual features of the reference image in each video frame and, when it finds them, moves the gaze point to the matching place on the photograph. Because every shopper's gaze ends up on the same image, heat maps, scan paths, and static areas of interest become possible; without mapping, each recording is a separate video with no shared coordinates. The result is only as good as its coverage: the mapping timeline turns green where the algorithm processed and applied the mapping, blue where it processed the frames but could not apply a mapping, and gray where nothing has run yet. Blue is not automatically a failure; the article notes that it may simply mean the respondent looked outside the frame of the reference image. The algorithm does fail when the image is absent from the video, when the gaze falls outside it, when the photograph is not similar enough to the scene, or when frames are blurred by fast movement. The glasses guide adds that mapping "relies exclusively on contrast," so a shelf lit differently on the field day than in the photograph maps poorly, and that mapped points "may look spatially closer than they were" because the scene camera sees more than the two dimensional image. Only the mapped portion of a recording contributes to AOI metrics, which is why the share of the shelf interval that mapped belongs in the methods.

AOIs are measurement decisions

An area of interest (AOI) is a region drawn on the reference image, such as the Sensodyne products, the POSM wing, each competitor block, and the price tags. Drawing it is a decision, and the decision should be made before data collection, on the AOI master list from section 02, so that the boundaries cannot drift toward the result you hope for (Simmons et al., 2011). Because no tracker is perfect, the boundary trades two errors against each other: draw it too small and real looks at the product fall outside (misses); draw it too large and looks at the neighbor fall inside (false alarms). Treating the AOI as a signal detection problem makes that trade explicit (Orquin et al., 2016), and noisy data need larger regions than clean data (Hessels et al., 2016). The iMotions AOI article passes on the handbook rule of about 1 to 1.5 degrees of visual angle of padding around an object, recommends simple shapes over tight polygons, and notes two mechanics that matter for small shelf elements: a fixation counts as inside an AOI if at least one of its samples is inside, so a fixation on the edge of two neighbors can count for both, and a dwell begins or ends only after the gaze has been inside or outside the region for the dwell classification threshold, 100 ms by default.

The primary test: Welch's t test on TTFF

In plain words

Two groups of shoppers saw two different shelves. Their average times to first fixation differ by 1.34 seconds. Could a difference that large arise by chance if POSM had no effect, just from which shoppers happened to be in which group? The t test answers by comparing the difference between the means with the spread inside the groups. Welch's version does not assume the two groups have the same spread, which is why it is the default here, and the p value is the probability of a difference at least this large if there were no effect.

The same test in symbols
t = (MPOSM − MNoPOSM) / √(s12/n1 + s22/n2)

Simulated result: MPOSM = 2.25 s (SD 0.86, n = 14), MNoPOSM = 3.59 s (SD 0.98, n = 15); difference 1.34 s, 95% CI [0.64, 2.04]; Welch t(26.89) = 3.91, p < .001 (the console prints p = 0.0006). R prints the statistic as negative because it subtracts NoPOSM from POSM; report the magnitude and state the direction in words. The degrees of freedom are fractional because Welch's formula adjusts them for unequal variances.

Effect size: Cohen's d with a confidence interval

A p value says whether a difference is surprising; it does not say how large it is. Cohen's d expresses the difference between the means in units of the pooled standard deviation, so a reader can compare it across studies and plan a replication (Lakens, 2013). For TTFF the pooled SD is about 0.92 s and d = 1.45 with a 95 percent confidence interval of [0.61, 2.26] from the noncentral t distribution. By the usual labels, 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large (Cohen, 1992), that is a large effect, but the interval is wide: with 14 and 15 shoppers the data are consistent with anything from a moderate effect to an enormous one. The simulated data were built to show a large effect so that it is visible with this sample; real shelf effects are usually smaller, and the width of the interval is the honest part of the report.

A nonparametric check

TTFF is bounded at zero and skewed to the right, and the groups are small, so the t test is repeated as a Wilcoxon rank sum test, which replaces the values with ranks and asks whether one group's ranks sit higher. If it agrees, the conclusion does not depend on normality. In the simulated data it does: W = 184, p < .001 for TTFF and W = 27, p < .001 for dwell share. The assumption checks also behaved: the variance ratio for TTFF was 1.31 and the Shapiro Wilk p values were .165 (NoPOSM) and .482 (POSM), neither flagging strong skew, although small samples give that test little power.

The secondary tests

Dwell share on Sensodyne_Products uses every kept respondent (never fixated AOIs count as zero): POSM M = 18.43 percent (SD 5.98) versus NoPOSM M = 10.76 percent (SD 4.42), a difference of 7.68 percentage points, 95% CI [3.62, 11.73], Welch t(23.87) = 3.91, p < .001 (console p = 0.0007), d = 1.47, 95% CI [0.63, 2.28]. Time on the two competitor AOIs combined, the comparison the 2020 report headlined, was lower with POSM: 36.0 s (SD 14.6) versus 49.3 s (SD 16.4), t(26.95) = 2.32, p = .028, d = 0.86, 95% CI [0.09, 1.62]. Because every kept respondent fixated Sensodyne_Products, TTFF max. equaled TTFF parent and gave the same test; with real data the two usually differ, and both are reported.

Why rows from one shopper are not independent, and the mixed model

The t test on one AOI uses one row per shopper and is fine. Pooling all 145 rows of the five shared AOIs into one test would treat each shopper as five independent people, which violates the independence assumption that every classical test makes: a shopper who looks longer at everything raises all five of her rows together. A linear mixed effects model handles this by giving each respondent a random intercept, a personal baseline that absorbs the shopper level tendency, while AOI, condition, and their interaction are the fixed effects of interest (Bates et al., 2015). The same logic argues for treating products as a random factor when a study samples many of them (Judd et al., 2012), and a tutorial on power for such models sets realistic expectations for a class sized sample (Brysbaert & Stevens, 2018). In the simulated data the model is dwell_pct ~ aoi * condition + (1 | respondent), fit with lme4 and lmerTest. The interaction answers "does the POSM effect differ by AOI?", and it does: F(4, 108) = 8.14, p < .001, with the model based means showing Sensodyne_Products rising from 10.8 to 18.4 percent and Competitor_B falling from 15.9 to 10.6. The random intercept SD was 3.20 percentage points against a residual SD of 4.79, so about 31 percent of the variance in dwell share lay between shoppers. The main effect of condition alone was not significant, F(1, 27) = 0.50, p = .487, which makes sense: POSM moved attention around the shelf rather than raising it everywhere.

The survey

Purchase intent for Sensodyne (1 to 7) was higher after the POSM shelf, M = 4.86 (SD 1.03), than after the NoPOSM shelf, M = 4.13 (SD 1.36), but the difference of 0.72 points had a 95% CI of [-0.19, 1.64], Welch t(25.95) = 1.63, p = .116, d = 0.60, 95% CI [-0.15, 1.34]. Brand recall (12 of 14 versus 8 of 15) and choosing a Sensodyne product (7 of 14 versus 3 of 15) ran in the same direction, with Fisher's exact test p = .109 and p = .120; Fisher's test is used instead of chi square because the counts are small. Across conditions, dwell share on Sensodyne_Products correlated with purchase intent, r = .49, 95% CI [.15, .73], p = .007. Attention and stated intent move together, but the survey effects are smaller and less certain, which is a recurring finding when biometrics and self report are collected side by side (Venkatraman et al., 2015).

Writing the results: numbers in sentences, tables, and figures that do not overclaim

A results section reports what was found, in the order the analysis plan promised, without interpreting it; the interpretation waits for the discussion. Readers and reviewers expect a fixed shape: a reminder of what was measured and on whom, the descriptive statistics, the test for each hypothesis, the effect size with its interval, and a table and figure with captions that stand on their own (Appelbaum et al., 2018). Every statistic in this section is simulated and is labeled as such; the form is what you are learning.

The shape of a results section

Open with one sentence that restates the sample after exclusions and the measures, so the reader does not have to look back ("Fourteen shoppers saw the POSM shelf and 15 the NoPOSM shelf; the primary measure was TTFF to Sensodyne_Products"). Then take the hypotheses in order. For each one, give the descriptives, the test, and the effect size in one or two sentences, and point to the table or figure. Report every pre-registered test, including the ones that did not reach significance, and report the sensitivity checks in a clause rather than a paragraph. Put secondary and exploratory results after the primary ones, and label them. Close with the self report measures and any relation between measures, which here is the correlation between dwell share and purchase intent.

APA style numbers

APA style has a small number of rules that make results readable. Report means and standard deviations as M = 2.25, SD = 0.86 with the unit stated once. Report a test as the statistic with its degrees of freedom in parentheses, then p, then the effect size with its interval: t(26.89) = 3.91, p < .001, d = 1.45, 95% CI [0.61, 2.26]. Give exact p values to two or three decimals (p = .116) and write p < .001 only when the value is smaller than that. Drop the leading zero for statistics that cannot exceed 1, such as p and r (r = .49), and keep it for those that can (d = 0.60). Use two decimals for most statistics, the sign that matches the direction you describe, and italics for the letters M, SD, t, p, d, and r when the journal asks. Say "did not reach significance" rather than "was not significant," and never write "trend toward significance."

Presenting heat maps and navigation paths honestly

The sample report's figures are a good model of how to show pictures from glasses data to a client, and a good test of what a picture can and cannot claim. A heat map aggregates every mapped gaze sample from every respondent in the analysis over the whole gaze mapped interval, so its caption must say which respondents (all 15 in the report; 14 or 15 per shelf in the simulated study) and which interval (the shelf interval, not the walk through the store). A navigation path is an average order of first looks; the report's page 14 shows that shoppers looked at Sensodyne products first with POSM and at competitor products first without, and it says "on average" because no single shopper followed exactly that path. The bar charts are honest in the same way: the report states that time on competitor products fell from 127.9 s without POSM to 94.3 s with POSM, "almost 25% less," that time on the SKU on the poster plus the SKU on the shelf was 2.8 s with POSM against 1.9 s without, and that on the wing the product image was noticed first (28 s, 100 percent to notice) and received 1.1 s against 0.3 s for the main message and 0.2 s for the sub message. Those numbers belong to the report's 15 Copenhagen shoppers in March 2020, with no test reported, and a results section that reuses them must say so. Place a heat map beside its AOI numbers, state the aggregation in the caption, and let the reader see that the red region and the tested AOI are the same place.

What a picture cannot say

A heat map cannot show that two shelves differ; it shows where one group looked. Two heat maps side by side invite the eye to compare, which is why the AOI numbers and their test must sit beside them. Scan paths show one respondent and should be labeled with that respondent's identifier and interval.

The client version

A client reads the executive summary and may stop there, so it carries the findings as short headlines with the direction and the size, and nothing a later page would contradict. The sample report's summary has five numbered headlines, from "POSM drives rapid notice of the product" to "product imagery on the wing may detract from key messaging," each supported by a page with a figure. Two habits make the academic and the client versions consistent: write the headline from the tested result, not from the heat map, and keep the hedge the data require ("may create a halo effect" in the report, "did not reach significance" in the paper).

Interpreting and publishing: what the finding means, what it cannot mean, and where it goes

The discussion is where the numbers become a claim. It answers the research question from section 01, says what the answer means for the client and for theory, lists the limitations that bound the claim, states what a replication should change, and places the work in a literature. It is also where overclaiming happens, so the structure below keeps each claim tied to a result.

What the finding means

For the client, the simulated result repeats the pattern the sample report described: with point of sale material present, shoppers fixated Sensodyne products about 1.3 seconds sooner, gave them a larger share of looking time, and spent less time on competitor packages, while purchase intent moved in the same direction with less certainty. The report's own interpretation is that the POSM may prime attention, directing the first looks to the advertised brand, and may create a halo effect, a more favorable response to the brand that spreads from the sign to the product; its "cognitive insight" box argues that congruent elements, sign plus product, make the supported information more salient. These are plausible mechanisms, and the honest word in each is "may." For theory, the result joins a literature that links attention at the shelf to choice: the number and position of shelf facings change brand attention and evaluation at the point of purchase (Chandon et al., 2009), in-store signage captures attention measured with glasses (Otterbring et al., 2014), shoppers in a real store rely on simple visual heuristics (Wästlund et al., 2015), merchandise displays draw attention in ways that differ between the laboratory and the field (Huddleston et al., 2015) (Huddleston et al., 2018), and attention on a desktop screen and at a supermarket shelf are related but not the same (Bialkova et al., 2020). Attention predicts choice more often than it explains it (Orquin & Mueller Loose, 2013), which is why the correlation between dwell share and intent (r = .49, simulated) is reported as an association and not as a path.

Limitations particular to a glasses study

Every limitation below should appear in the discussion of a real version of this study, in roughly this order of threat. First, gaze mapping coverage: only the mapped portion of each recording contributes to the AOI metrics, so changes in lighting, blurred frames, and moments when the shopper turned away reduce the data, and the Valid data figures and a visual check of the mapped replay should be reported with the results; the one exclusion in the simulated study shows how a slipped frame can invalidate a session (Niehorster et al., 2020). Second, fixation classification on glasses data mixes head and eye movement, and iMotions itself notes that gaze based metrics can be preferred for mobile recordings, so the fixation based results should be checked against gaze based dwell and hit time. Third, the sample is small and the design is between subjects: the large simulated effects carry wide intervals (for TTFF, d between roughly 0.6 and 2.3), and the survey outcomes had too little power to decide anything. Fourth, one store, one shelf, one brand, and a POSM wing that also changed the physical layout, so the effect of the message cannot be separated from the extra space and color the wing added, and shelf position was not controlled (Gidlöf et al., 2017). Fifth, demand effects: shoppers who wear glasses and are asked to shop a category know they are observed and may inspect the shelf more thoroughly than usual, inflating dwell times and perhaps compressing the difference between conditions. Sixth, AOI boundaries: small elements such as the wing text need margins sized to the tracker's accuracy, and a different margin would change the numbers (Hessels et al., 2016). The validity checklist of stimulus, task, participant, and apparatus threats is the frame reviewers use (Orquin & Holmqvist, 2018); a discussion that walks through it in that order rarely misses one.

What a replication would change

A replication would be preregistered: the hypotheses, the AOI master list, the exclusion rule, the primary and secondary measures, and the tests would be written and time stamped before the first shopper, so that the analysis cannot bend toward the result (Nosek et al., 2018). It would recruit enough shoppers for the smallest effect worth detecting rather than for the one the pilot happened to show, following the power calculation from section 02 (Cohen, 1992) (Faul et al., 2007), and would recruit 20 to 25 percent extra for data loss. It would add a second store and counterbalance shelf position, add a familiarity item to the survey as a covariate (Otterbring et al., 2016), and report gaze based metrics beside fixation based ones. It would also report mapping coverage per respondent, because a reviewer who cannot see coverage cannot judge the metrics.

Ethics and privacy of the recordings

A glasses recording is a video of a public place with other people in it, plus a stream of gaze data that can reveal more about the wearer than the wearer expects: interests, reading ability, and in some cases health, which is why privacy scholars treat eye tracking as sensitive data (Kröger et al., 2020). The ethics of consumer neuroscience add the question of what a company may do with attention data that participants did not know they were giving (Stanton et al., 2017), and the industry code the lab follows sets out consent, privacy, and participant rights (Neuromarketing Science & Business Association, n.d.). In practice: consent that names the scene video and the gaze data, the store's permission, the Video Face Blur post process from section 05 for bystanders, a retention period written into the IRB protocol, and no recording shared outside the study without the participant's agreement.

Where this kind of work is published, and what reviewers ask for

A shelf study with a managerial contribution fits the Journal of Retailing or the Journal of Business Research; one framed around consumer psychology, priming or halo mechanisms, fits Psychology and Marketing; one about the POSM as an advertising execution fits the Journal of Advertising; and a paper whose contribution is the method itself, for example a validation of gaze mapping coverage or a comparison of fixation and gaze based metrics on glasses data, fits Behavior Research Methods, where the eye tracking methodology papers cited in this workshop appeared. Reviewers in all of them ask the same questions: were the hypotheses and analysis set in advance; are the apparatus, filter, mapping, and AOI decisions reported so the study can be repeated; is the effect size given with an interval; are the exclusions counted and justified; and do the conclusions stay inside the design (Orquin & Holmqvist, 2018) (Appelbaum et al., 2018). A reviewer will also ask whether the biometric measure added anything a survey could not have given (Venkatraman et al., 2015), so say what the glasses showed that the questionnaire did not: here, where the first look went and how long before it went there.

A model discussion paragraph (simulated)

Model paragraph

In these simulated data, point of sale material changed where shoppers looked first and how they divided their attention across the shelf: Sensodyne products were fixated about 1.3 seconds sooner, received a larger share of looking time, and drew attention away from competitor packages, which is the pattern the iMotions In-Store Shopper Report described for its Copenhagen sample. The self report measures moved in the same direction but with smaller and less certain effects, a reminder that attention and stated intent are related but not interchangeable and that fifteen respondents per shelf give little power for survey outcomes. Several limitations are typical of mobile eye tracking in a store and would apply to a real version of this study. Every AOI metric depends on gaze mapping, so coverage and the Valid data metric should be reported alongside the results; fixation classification on glasses data mixes head and eye movement, so the fixation based results should be checked against gaze based dwell and hit time; the sample is small and between subjects, so the large effect sizes carry wide confidence intervals and a replication with more shoppers per shelf is needed before any practical recommendation; the data come from one store, one shelf, and one brand, and the wing also changed the shelf's layout; and wearing glasses while shopping a named category creates demand effects that may inflate dwell times. These numbers are simulated for teaching; they demonstrate the analysis, not the effect.

The key takeaways slide

The client deck ends where the paper's discussion begins. The report's Key Takeaways page states three conclusions, each paired with a recommendation: POSM is an effective technique to drive attention to the brand and reduces time with competitor packages; without POSM, competitor packages are noticed sooner, so Sensodyne's packaging alone may not drive attention; and messaging on the wing may go missed because the product image is larger and more central than the text, so text size and placement should be optimized. Notice the shape: finding, mechanism, action. The page before it pairs a heat map with a "cognitive insight" box that interprets the path to the product; that layout, a figure with its interpretation beside it, is worth copying in a presentation, as long as the interpretation is labeled as interpretation.

Common mistake

Writing the discussion from the heat map. The heat map suggested the story; the AOI test carried it. Every sentence that makes a claim should be traceable to a statistic in the results or to a cited source, and the limitations paragraph should be as long as the implications paragraph.

Interpret a shelf finding for a client and for theory, list the limitations of a glasses study in order of threat, say what a preregistered replication would change, handle the privacy of the recordings, and choose a journal by the contribution.
