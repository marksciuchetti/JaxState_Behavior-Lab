From question to publication with the Aurora: testing two television commercials

This workshop follows one advertising question from the first sentence a client says to the last sentence of a written results and discussion section. You will plan a test of two television commercials, build it in iMotions Lab 11.1, set up the Smart Eye Aurora and calibrate each viewer (a short routine that teaches the tracker that viewer's eyes), measure attention on moving video, test three hypotheses, and write the result for a journal and for a client. Every section explains why a step exists before it shows how.

Screen based eye tracking records where on a screen a viewer looks, and when. The Aurora, a slim bar mounted under the lab monitor (Smart Eye, n.d.), lights the eyes with infrared light and finds two landmarks in each eye image: the center of the pupil and a small reflection of the light on the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye. As the eye turns, the pupil moves while the reflection stays nearly still, so the offset between them gives the direction of gaze. The method is called pupil center corneal reflection (PCCR) (Duchowski, 2017). At the lab Aurora's sampling rate of 60 Hz, one gaze sample (one estimate of the point on the screen where the eyes are directed) arrives every 16.67 milliseconds. Because the screen is fixed and every viewer sees the same frame at the same moment, each sample already has its place on the stimulus. If you did Workshop 1, notice what is gone: no gaze mapping, the step that projected each wearer's moving scene video onto a photograph of the shelf.

The method shows where and when, not why, and not how much the viewer liked what was seen (Wedel & Pieters, 2008); attention predicts choice without measuring it (Orquin & Mueller Loose, 2013). Commercials make a natural second study because attention is the gate that every other advertising effect passes through. The brand, the pictures, and the text of an ad compete for the eye (Pieters & Wedel, 2004), and fixations on the brand feed later memory for it (Wedel & Pieters, 2000). When the brand appears affects whether viewers who can avoid a commercial stay with it (Teixeira, Wedel, & Pieters, 2010), and viewers who fast forward keep their eyes near the center of the screen, where brand information can still get through (Brasel & Gips, 2008).

The worked example is real material: the iMotions demonstration study "DEMO - SonyBravia" in the lab's Library, built around two Sony BRAVIA commercials, "Balls" and "Paint". A SIMULATED data set of 30 viewers carries the numbers. The Aurora works with either lab computer, because both license keys include the Eye Tracking Screen based module, and its Smart Eye setup is made once for each computer and monitor.

01

From idea to research question

A client rarely arrives with a research question. A client arrives with a business question, a statement of what the business wants to know or to happen. Picture a brand manager with two finished commercials for the same television set and money to air one: "Which of our two commercials does more for the product?" The sentence names a wish and a comparison, but not who will watch, what "does more" means, how it will be measured, or what difference would count. A research question names the problem the study will answer and signals the type of study needed. A hypothesis is a testable answer to that question, written with the specific variables that will be measured. The iMotions design guide asks for both before any data are collected, because writing the expected answer first forces you to say what you would accept as evidence and guards against confirmation bias, the habit of seeing what you expected to see.

The independent variable is what the researcher changes; the dependent variable is what is measured in response. Here the independent variable is the commercial, with two levels, Balls and Paint. The lab asks every study to pass through one sentence with four slots: who, sees what, measured how, compared against what. For the ad test it reads: adult television viewers (who) watch both commercials on the lab monitor, in an order balanced across viewers (sees what), while the Aurora records where they look and a rating slide after each commercial asks how much they liked it (measured how), and each viewer's response to Paint is compared with the same viewer's response to Balls (against what). If you cannot fill all four slots, you do not yet have a study.

Weak questions fail in three ways, and each can be repaired. "Which commercial is better?" is an opinion and too general: better at what, and for whom? "Does Paint work?" is missing its what and its how: work at what, shown by which metric, against what? "What do people look at in commercials?" has everything as its answer, so nothing can be tested. The repair names an observable, a measure, and a comparison: "Among the same viewers, is the share of the product shots spent fixating the television set higher in Paint than in Balls?" The iMotions table below makes the same contrast.

Before choosing measures, separate the three outcomes an ad test can measure, because they are different things and they can disagree. Attention is where and when the eyes go: whether the viewer fixated the product, how soon the logo was found. Affect is how the viewer feels about the commercial, here how much it is liked. Memory is what the viewer can retrieve afterward, such as the brand name in an unaided recall question (asked without showing the brand) at the end of the session. A commercial can hold the eyes and be disliked, or be enjoyed and leave the brand forgotten. The eye tracker measures only attention: liking needs the rating slide, and memory needs a question after the commercials. A large comparison of traditional, eye tracking, physiological, and brain measures as predictors of how ads performed in the market found that the newer measures did not all add the same thing (Venkatraman et al., 2015), so name the single outcome each measure stands for.

Attention metrics answer where, when, how long, how often, and in what order. They do not answer why. A long look at the television set can mean interest, confusion, or admiration for the picture, and the recording looks the same in each case. Reading a mental state straight off a physiological signal is called reverse inference, and reviews of consumer neuroscience warn against it (Plassmann, Venkatraman, Huettel, & Yoon, 2015).

Now the hypotheses, written before any data exist. They need three more terms. An area of interest (AOI) is a named region of the stimulus where iMotions computes metrics; on a video it is a dynamic AOI, which follows its element as it moves and counts only while the element is on screen. A fixation is a period, typically 100 to 300 milliseconds, in which the eyes rest on one spot.

  • H1. Dwell time (%) on the Product_TV AOI (the television set in the product shots) is higher in Paint than in Balls. Dwell time (%) is the share of the time the AOI was on screen that the viewer spent fixating inside it.
  • H2. The time to first fixation (TTFF) on the Brand_Logo AOI, the logo on the end card (the closing frame that shows the brand), is shorter in Paint than in Balls. TTFF is a family of metrics that differ in when the clock starts: TTFF AOI counts from the moment the AOI becomes active, TTFF parent from the start of the commercial, and TTFF max. gives a viewer who never fixated the AOI the parent's full duration. H2 uses TTFF AOI, so its clock starts when the logo appears and stops at the first fixation on it.
  • H3. Liking, rated from 1 to 7 after each commercial, differs between the two commercials.

All three tests in this study are two tailed. A two tailed test asks whether two means differ in either direction. A one tailed test looks only in the predicted direction, so it is blind to a difference the other way, and it is defensible only when a difference the other way would be meaningless. Here it would not be: if Balls held more attention on the product than Paint, or if viewers found the logo sooner in Balls, the client would want to know. So H1 and H2 each state the direction that the reasoning predicts, and their tests are still two tailed. H3 states no direction at all, because nothing in the attention argument says which commercial will be liked more (a spectacle can be enjoyed for itself), and its test is two tailed as well. The directions of H1 and H2 come from reasons. Pictures capture attention whatever their size, while the brand passes attention on to the other elements (Pieters & Wedel, 2004), and visual complexity changes an ad's stopping power, its ability to catch and hold the eye (Pieters, Wedel, & Batra, 2010). In this workshop's simulated plan (a plan, not a measurement of the real commercials), the hero element, the balls or the paint, is on screen for 40 seconds in Balls and 32 in Paint, and the product shots for 14 and 20 seconds. Paint leaves the spectacle less time to compete with the product (H1), and eyes already on the product may find the logo sooner (H2). Viewers also differ in how far into an ad they go (Rosbergen, Pieters, & Wedel, 1997), so averages hide differences between people; a review of eye movements on advertisements summarizes this research (Higgins, Leinenger, & Rayner, 2014). A hypothesis grounded in a reason is one you can learn from when it fails.

A study run "just to see what we can see" will see something

The iMotions design guide puts it plainly: "Biometric measures are granular enough that you can almost always find some result." The AOI Metrics article lists 48 metrics for every AOI, so four AOIs in two commercials give 384 numbers for every viewer. Test the 48 metrics on each of the four AOIs for a difference between the commercials and you have run 192 tests; if no real difference existed anywhere, about one test in twenty, roughly ten in all, would still come out significant at the .05 level.

The remedy is to choose one primary metric for each hypothesis before the data exist and to label everything else secondary or exploratory. Here the primary metrics are Dwell time (%) on Product_TV for H1, TTFF AOI on Brand_Logo for H2, and the 1 to 7 liking rating for H3. Write them into a dated plan with the hypotheses, the sample size, and the exclusion rule. Filing that plan before collection is called preregistration, and it lets a reader tell a prediction from a story told afterward. Section 02 adds the design, section 03 writes the whole plan, and section 08 holds you to it.

02

Designing a within subjects test

A design is the set of decisions that make a comparison fair, and the first is how to assign viewers to conditions. In a between subjects design each participant experiences one condition; in a within subjects design every participant experiences all conditions. The iMotions design guide states the trade: between subjects designs need larger samples, while within subjects designs need fewer participants but risk fatigue. Within subjects also makes each viewer his or her own control, so stable differences between people (some viewers look at products in any commercial) drop out of the comparison.

If you did Workshop 1, you met the other choice: a real shelf could not be reset in front of a shopper, because the first visit taught her where the brand sat, so the shelf study was between subjects. A screen can show two different commercials in one session. Within subjects is still wrong when the first condition spoils the second, as with two cuts of one commercial that differ only in the end card: by the second viewing the story is known and the change may be spotted, so that comparison goes back to between subjects.

The price of within subjects is that the first commercial can change how the second is watched. Carryover is what the first condition leaves behind: knowledge of the brand, the expectation of a product shot, or tiredness. An order effect is a difference caused by position alone, such as more looking at the product in whichever commercial comes second. Neither can be removed, but both can be balanced. Counterbalancing presents each order to equal numbers of viewers so that, in the design guide's words, order effects cancel each other out. Order A shows Balls first; Order B shows Paint first.

In iMotions each order is a flow, an ordered list of the stimuli a respondent sees. A study can hold one flow or several, and the study settings assign respondents to flows manually or at random (section 04 builds them). Manual allocation from a prepared list (A, B, A, B) guarantees equal groups; random allocation keeps the operator's choices out. The alternative to flows is a test plan, a file listing each respondent and the stimuli he or she will see. Either way, record the order as a variable, because section 08 checks whether it mattered.

Paired data are powerful. A viewer who looks at products a lot in one commercial tends to do so in the other; in the simulated data of section 08 a viewer's two product dwell shares correlate at r = .48 (simulated). The paired t test works on each viewer's difference, Paint minus Balls, so the stable part of each viewer cancels. The effect size states how large a difference is in standard units; for paired data it is dz, the mean of the differences divided by their standard deviation. The more a viewer's two measures move together, the smaller that standard deviation and the larger dz (Lakens, 2013). Power is the probability of detecting an effect of a given size if it is real; the usual target is 80 percent.

In plain words

For a large effect, dz = 0.8, a paired test at two tailed alpha .05 and 80 percent power needs 15 usable viewers; a medium effect, dz = 0.5, needs 34, and a small one, dz = 0.3, needs 90. If you did Workshop 1, compare: the same large effect between subjects needed 26 shoppers per group, and a medium effect between subjects needs 64 per group.

The same calculation in R and G*Power
R: power.t.test(delta = 0.5, sd = 1, sig.level = .05, power = .80, type = "paired") returns n = 33.4, rounded up to 34. G*Power, Means: difference between two dependent means (matched pairs), two tails, dz = 0.5, alpha .05, power .80: total sample size 34.

G*Power is the program most researchers cite for this (Faul, Erdfelder, Lang, & Buchner, 2007). The conventions 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 large were written for differences between groups (Cohen, 1992), so they are only a rough guide for dz.

Three practical rules follow. The iMotions design guide recommends 20 to 60 people per study condition, and its Static Image Testing template plans 25 per cell. The lab's convention for a screen study is 25 to 30 usable viewers. Recruit 20 to 25 percent more than the usable target, because calibration fails for some viewers and some recordings fall below the exclusion rule: 30 usable viewers means scheduling about 36 to 38.

Two commercials are two stimuli, two specific things shown to viewers, not a sample of commercials in general, and a difference between them belongs to everything that differs at once: story, music, product shots, end card. The result can say that Paint, as made, held more of these viewers' attention on the product than Balls, as made. It cannot say that spectacle hurts product attention; that needs many commercials analyzed as a sample, called stimulus sampling (Judd, Westfall, & Kenny, 2012), with power that depends on the number of stimuli as well as viewers (Brysbaert & Stevens, 2018).

Each slide in the session has a job. Calibration, in which the viewer follows dots so that the tracker learns his or her eyes, is part of every session (section 05). A Welcome slide greets the viewer. An Instructions slide gives the task in the same words to everyone: "Watch the screen as you would watch television at home." It must not name the brand or the comparison, because a viewer who guesses what the researcher hopes to see tends to supply it, a demand effect. Before each commercial comes a baseline, a neutral rest screen that lets each commercial start from the same calm state and, in Workshops 3 and 4, gives other sensors a resting level. After each commercial comes a rating slide, a survey slide (a screen that asks a question) where the viewer rates liking from 1 to 7.

Two more decisions are written now. The exclusion rule: a viewer whose Valid data (the percentage of expected gaze samples the tracker delivered) is below 70 percent is excluded, and every viewer's calibration result (Excellent, Good, or Poor) and recalibrations go into the session log. Calibration and the viewer's eyes both affect data quality (Nyström, Andersson, Holmqvist, & van de Weijer, 2013), and a rule chosen after seeing who looks bad is chosen to get a result. Then the AOI master list: dynamic AOIs are drawn after collection but planned now from the storyboard, the shot by shot plan of the commercial. Informed consent is a documented explanation, given before participation, of the five items the iMotions consent FAQ lists: the purpose, that taking part is voluntary and can be withdrawn, the participant's rights, how the data will be handled, and the risks. What a person looks at can reveal interests, health, and identity (Kröger, Lutz, & Müller, 2020), and the face video of Workshop 3 identifies the viewer outright. 03

The worked example in full

Everything in sections 01 and 02 fits on one page, and writing that page is the first deliverable of any study in this lab. Below is the commercial test as a student study plan, following the design of the simulated data set. Read it as a template: change the commercials, the AOIs, and the numbers, and you have the plan for your own test.

Question
Among adult viewers, which of two commercials for the same television brand, Balls or Paint, holds more attention on the product and on the brand at the end, and which is liked more?
Hypotheses
H1: Dwell time (%) on Product_TV is higher in Paint than in Balls. H2: TTFF AOI (ms) on Brand_Logo is shorter in Paint. H3: liking (1 to 7) differs between the commercials, two tailed.
Design
Within subjects, one factor (commercial) with two levels. Two flows for counterbalancing, Order A (Balls first) and Order B (Paint first), allocated alternately from a list prepared before collection; the order is stored as Group (A or B).
Stimuli and flow
Commercial_1_Balls and Commercial_2_Paint, 60 s each in the simulation. Welcome, Instructions, then for each commercial a Baseline slide, the commercial, and a rating slide.
AOIs
Four dynamic AOIs with the active intervals planned in section 02: Product_TV (14 s in Balls, 20 s in Paint), Brand_Logo (last 5 s), Tagline_Text (last 4 s), and Hero_Element (40 s in Balls, 32 s in Paint). The list is final before collection.
Sample
30 viewers scheduled (R01 to R30), 15 per order, for a target of at least 25 usable; 15 usable viewers give 80 percent power for dz = 0.8.
Task
"Watch the screen as you would watch television at home," read in the same words to every viewer.
Measures
Eye tracking, per viewer, commercial, and AOI: Dwell time (%), TTFF AOI (ms), respondent ratio, fixation count, and revisit count. Survey: liking of each commercial (1 to 7) on its rating slide; at the end, unaided brand recall (Yes or No) and the preferred commercial.
Exclusion rule
Exclude a viewer whose Valid data is below 70 percent. Log every viewer's calibration result and recalibrations.
Analysis plan
For each hypothesis, a two tailed paired t test on the Paint minus Balls differences, with dz and its 95 percent confidence interval, checked by a Wilcoxon signed rank test. H2 on the viewers with a TTFF AOI value in both commercials, plus a sensitivity analysis for viewers who never fixated the logo. A mixed model of Product_TV dwell share with commercial, order position, their interaction, and a random intercept per viewer, to check the order effect. Brand recall against logo dwell time with a point biserial correlation. Alpha .05.
Deliverables
An AOI table by commercial, heatmaps for named moments, three figures, a results section and a discussion in journal form, and a three sentence client summary.

Three names in the analysis plan return in section 08: the Wilcoxon signed rank test checks the paired t test using the ranks of the differences instead of their values; the mixed model is a regression that knows which rows come from the same viewer; the point biserial correlation relates a measurement to a yes or no answer.

Each hypothesis has one primary measure, and the pairing is the heart of the plan. H1 uses Dwell time (%) on Product_TV, a share rather than milliseconds, because the television set is on screen for 14 seconds in Balls and 20 in Paint; a share of the active time puts both on one scale. H2 uses TTFF AOI (ms) on Brand_Logo, timed from the moment the logo appears, with the respondent ratio (the percentage of viewers who fixated the AOI at all) as a check that a faster time does not come from fewer viewers looking. H3 uses the liking ratings, which no gaze metric can supply. Brand recall and the preferred commercial are secondary measures of memory and affect, and Tagline_Text and Hero_Element are described, not tested.

Windows scaling distorts auto detection (a 1920 x 1080 monitor at 125 percent reads as 1536 x 864), and survey slides need Display Scaling at 100 percent, so set 100 percent first. You should see the monitor's real size and native resolution. Common mistake. Accepting the laptop's own screen.
  4. Name the study on the "Name your study" page by the lab convention YYYY_Client_Topic (for example 2026_ClassPractice_TwoCommercials) and finish the wizard. You should see the study in the Library. Common mistake. Keeping the default timestamp name.

4.2 Build the flow for Order A

The flow is the session in order: Welcome, Instructions, a Baseline (a neutral rest screen before each commercial), the first commercial and its rating slide, a second Baseline, the second commercial and its rating slide, and a thank you. The DEMO - SonyBravia study uses names such as Baseline1, Commercial1A_Balls, and Commercial1A_Balls_Rating, which suggest the same pattern; open the study on the lab laptop to see its flow.

  1. Open the builder. Select your study and click Edit stimuli. You should see the Flow designer tab (Study flows, Repository, and Properties panels; tiles for Block, Media, Survey, Screen recording, Website, and Qualtrics) and the Stimuli overview tab. Common mistake. Editing the demo study instead of your own.
  2. Add the media. Click Media and add the two commercials. The manual prints the video formats as "MP4 or WAV", probably a misprint, so play every video in the study preview. If one lags, flickers, or has poor sound, right-click the study in the Library, choose "Convert videos for compatibility", and preview again. You should see smooth playback. Common mistake. Expecting a new file type: the command replaces the original and does not change the format.
  3. Add the rating slides. Click Survey, add a Scale element for liking from 1 to 7, give it a Question ID that names the commercial (Liking_Balls, Liking_Paint), and click Save to Study. A survey slide asks a question on screen; the Question ID labels the answer in the survey results. Do not randomize questions or labels, which ties all video replays to one image. You should see the slide in the flow. Common mistake. Two slides with the same Question ID.

The Aurora and the session

The Aurora is a bar under the monitor that lights the eyes with infrared light and films them. Its software finds the pupil center and the reflection of the light on the cornea (the clear front of the eye) and computes where the eye points, a method called pupil center corneal reflection (PCCR). The lab's unit records 60 gaze samples (estimated gaze points) per second, one every 16.67 ms; that is its sampling rate (Smart Eye, n.d.). Unlike the Neon in Workshop 1, it must be told where the screen is and must learn each viewer's eyes.

5.1 The Aurora: one setup per computer and monitor

Why: the tracker measures the eyes relative to itself, and a setup tells it where it sits on the screen. A new setup is required "if Windows Display Settings are changed or a different eye tracker model is used", so a new monitor or scaling value means a new setup.

  1. Install. The box holds the tracker, a mount with adhesive strips, and a USB cable. Install the Smart Eye Tracker software (the article names version 13.0.1 and Windows 10 or newer) and note the version on each lab computer.
  2. Start a setup. Open the Smart Eye Setup Tool, select Aurora, and click "+New setup". Select the stimulus monitor and click "Show center marker" (the manual's text spells it "Show center maker"; use the wording on the screen). You should see a "Center of display" mark at the bottom of that screen.
  3. Mount. Attach the mount flat and stable at the bottom center of the bezel (the bezel must be at least 14 mm), just below the display without covering it, and seat the Aurora so the square pegs fit the square slots, glass toward the viewer. Common mistake. A mount that is off center or tilted.
  4. Align and save. Press any key, click Next, drag the two guidelines to the ends of the tracker, and press Enter. Name the setup after the monitor and click Finish. You should see the setup in the tool's list.
  5. Connect. Plug in the USB cable, close the Setup Tool, and open Preferences > Global Settings (Alt+G) > Eye tracking tab. Check Eye Tracking Server and choose the Smart Eye entry in Model (the manuals spell it "Smart Eye AI-X/Aurora" and "Smart Eye Aurora or Smart Eye AI-X"; verify on the lab machine). 5.2 The viewer

The planning article's rules are short. No sunlight, and no fluorescent office lights. A chair without moving parts, the head about 60 cm from the screen, the eyes near the middle of the screen. The widget, which the planning article calls the Eye Finder (double-click detaches it), shows the sample rate and the distance, and the position is right when the two dotted squares merge. Glasses and contact lenses can be tracked but take longer to calibrate; thick rims, strong prescriptions, astigmatism, and laser surgery make tracking hard. Head movement and changes in distance lower data quality even for trackers built to tolerate them (Niehorster, Cornelissen, Holmqvist, Hooge, & Hessels, 2018), hence the fixed chair.

5.3 Calibration

Why: eyes differ, so before recording the viewer looks at known points and the tracker learns how that viewer's eye images map onto the screen. Recalibrating again and again; a tired viewer calibrates worse.

06

From gaze samples to metrics: what a 60 Hz screen based tracker measures, and how to describe it

Every number in the results ends a chain: the Aurora records gaze samples, a filter sorts them into fixations and saccades, dynamic areas of interest mark where and when each element is on screen, and AOI metrics summarize what landed inside. The reporting standards ask for the apparatus, the processing, and the exclusion rules (Appelbaum et al., 2018), and the handbooks supply the vocabulary (Holmqvist et al., 2011) (Duchowski, 2017).

Gaze samples at 60 Hz

The Aurora lights the eyes with infrared light and locates the pupil and the corneal reflection, the pupil center corneal reflection principle. Each estimate of where the viewer looks is one gaze sample. At the lab unit's sampling rate of 60 Hz (Smart Eye, n.d.), a sample arrives every 16.67 ms, so a 60 s commercial yields about 3,600 samples per viewer and a 100 ms fixation, the short end of the typical 100 to 300 ms range, spans about six. If you did Workshop 1, note that a fixed screen needs no gaze mapping: every viewer sees the same frame at the same moment.

Accuracy and precision

In plain words

Accuracy: does the tracker point to the right place? Gaze recorded a centimeter left of the dot a viewer stares at is inaccurate, even if every sample lands on one spot. Precision: does it point to the same place twice? Samples scattered around the dot are imprecise, even if their average is on target.

The definitions in the literature

Accuracy is the average offset between measured and true gaze position, in degrees of visual angle. Precision is the spread of repeated samples while the eye is still, reported as the root mean square of sample to sample distances or as their standard deviation (Holmqvist et al., 2012).

The iMotions manuals define neither term. Both suffer with a poor calibration, glasses, contact lenses, and some eye physiology (Nyström et al., 2013), and with head movement and a poor seating distance (Niehorster et al., 2018). iMotions reports a calibration label (Excellent, Good, or Poor) and Valid data, the percentage of collected and interpolated samples, neither an accuracy in degrees; whether the Smart Eye software reports one must be verified on the lab machine.

Fixations, saccades, and the I-VT filter

A fixation is a period in which the eyes rest on one location long enough to take in detail; a saccade is the fast jump between fixations. Researchers disagree on the definitions (Hessels et al., 2018), so a methods section gives an operational one: whatever the named filter, with its listed parameters, classified as a fixation. Filters threshold velocity or dispersion (Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000).

The lab uses I-VT, velocity threshold identification: samples slower than the threshold form fixations, faster ones saccades. Velocity is measured in degrees of visual angle per second, an angle that depends on the distance to the screen, which is why the I-VT article calls the screen parameters "absolutely crucial."

The study's GAZE ANALYSIS tab holds Window Length, Velocity Threshold, Gap Fill-in with Max Gap Length, Noise Reduction (which can remove signal at low sampling rates), Discard Short Fixations with Minimum Fixation Duration, and Merge Adjacent Fixations. The article states no defaults and calls I-VT "typically used for higher-sampling eye tracking data (200 Hz or more)," with no rule for 60 Hz. So report the filter and every parameter exactly as the tab shows them, use the same settings for every respondent, and never interpret a single short fixation.

Gaze smoothing, a tick box in the Visualization menu (named Visualizations in the Gaze smoothing article; verify on the lab machine) that averages the drawn gaze over 160 ms (about 9 or 10 samples), is "exclusively for visualization purposes" and never changes fixations or exports.

Dynamic AOIs are measurement decisions

An area of interest (AOI) is a region whose gaze iMotions summarizes. Commercial elements move and come and go, so the AOIs are dynamic, with active intervals on a timeline and key frames that move the shape with the element. The AOI master list fixes them in advance: in the simulated design Product_TV is active 14 s in Balls and 20 s in Paint, Brand_Logo the last 5 s, Tagline_Text the last 4 s, and Hero_Element 40 s and 32 s respectively. A tight shape misses real looks and a loose one catches the neighbors', the trade that treating AOIs as a signal detection problem makes explicit (Orquin et al., 2016); the Areas of Interest article suggests padding of about 1 to 1.5 degrees, roughly 1 to 2 cm at 60 cm. For a small logo, a fixation counts as inside if one of its samples is, and a dwell begins only after the dwell classification threshold, 100 ms by default.

The metrics, as iMotions defines them

In the AOI Metrics article, "parent" means the stimulus holding the AOI, here the commercial. Time to first fixation (TTFF) has three versions.

H1: the paired t test

In plain words

For each viewer, subtract the Balls value from the Paint value. With no real difference, these would average about zero. The paired t test asks whether the average difference is far from zero relative to how much the differences vary. It is a one sample t test on the differences, so a viewer who looks at everything a lot cancels out in the subtraction.

The same test in symbols
t = Md / (SDd / √n), df = n − 1; dz = Md / SDd, so t = dz × √n

Simulated H1: Md = 7.25 percentage points (Paint minus Balls), SDd = 7.40, n = 29, so t(28) = 5.27, p < .001; 95% CI of the difference [4.43, 10.06].

If you did Workshop 1, you used Welch's test, which compares two independent groups; here both columns come from the same 29 people in a within subjects design, and a viewer's two dwell shares correlate, r = .48 (simulated). The paired test removes that shared part from the error, the source of its power. In Excel it is one argument: T.TEST type 1 (paired) instead of type 3.

The effect size for a paired test is dz, the mean difference divided by the SD of the differences (Lakens, 2013). For H1, dz = 0.98 with a 95 percent confidence interval of [0.53, 1.42] from the noncentral t distribution, a large effect by the usual labels (Cohen, 1992); Paint was higher for 25 of 29 viewers (simulated). Its denominator is the spread of differences, not of scores, so dz is not comparable to a between subjects d. The Wilcoxon signed rank test, which ranks the differences instead of assuming they are normal, agreed: V = 402, p < .001.

H2: the logo and the missing TTFF problem

The logo was fixated by 27 of 29 viewers (93.1 percent) in each commercial. A pair needs a TTFF in both, so the test uses 25 viewers: TTFF AOI was shorter for Paint (M = 786.96 ms, SD = 192.27) than for Balls (M = 906.76 ms, SD = 242.15), a difference of -119.80 ms, 95% CI [-256.30, 16.70], but it did not reach significance, t(24) = -1.81, p = .083, dz = -0.36, 95% CI [-0.76, 0.05]. The Wilcoxon test gave V = 88, p = .046. When two tests fall on opposite sides of .05, report both with the interval and call the result uncertain (simulated).

Three codings of never fixated answer three questions. Complete pairs ask how fast viewers who noticed the logo in both commercials noticed it. The sensitivity analysis H2b keeps all 29 by coding a never fixated logo as 5,000 ms, the end card's length: the difference stays similar, -124.24 ms, but the SD of the differences grows from 330.69 to 1,635.97 ms and the test loses its precision, t(28) = -0.41, p = .686 (simulated). TTFF max. would count from the commercial's start, with 60,000 ms for never, a third question. Report the chosen coding and its alternatives.

H3: liking on a 7 point scale

Liking was higher for Balls (M = 5.52, SD = 0.91) than for Paint (M = 5.00, SD = 1.13), t(28) = -2.56, p = .016, dz = -0.47, 95% CI [-0.86, -0.09]. Ratings are whole numbers and 17 of 29 viewers gave both commercials the same one, so their differences departed from normality (Shapiro Wilk p < .001) and the Wilcoxon test is reported beside the t test, V = 9, p = .017. The product dwell difference and the liking difference were unrelated, r = .04, p = .844 (simulated).

The order effect and the mixed model

The commercial seen second can benefit from the first, a carryover that counterbalancing spreads evenly over both commercials. A linear mixed effects model measures it: dwell_pct ~ commercial * order_position + (1 | respondent) (Bates et al., 2015), with fixed effects coded -0.5 and +0.5 and a random intercept as each viewer's personal baseline. The commercial effect was b = 7.37, SE = 1.21, t(27) = 6.07, p < .001. The order effect was b = 3.61, SE = 1.21, t(27) = 2.97, p = .006, 95% CI [1.12, 6.10]. The interaction did not reach significance, b = 1.87, p = .691. The random intercept SD was 5.36 against a residual SD of 4.62, an intraclass correlation (ICC) of .574 (.185 without the fixed effects): more than half of the remaining variance lay between viewers (simulated). Two commercials are two stimuli; generalizing to commercials would need many of them as a random factor (Judd et al., 2012) and a sample sized for that (Brysbaert & Stevens, 2018).

Recall, assumptions, and figures

Recall is one answer per viewer, so logo attention is each viewer's total Brand_Logo Dwell time (ms). Its correlation with recall coded 0 or 1, the point biserial correlation, was rpb(27) = .42, 95% CI [.06, .68], p = .025; 24 of 29 viewers (82.8 percent) recalled the brand. QQ plots and Shapiro Wilk tests showed no departure from normality for H1 (p = .970), H2 (p = .676), the model residuals (p = .806), or the intercepts (p = .976); H3 departed, as whole number ratings must (simulated).

Writing the results: paired statistics in sentences, a table, and honest pictures of video

A results section reports what was found in the planned order and leaves interpretation to the discussion. Readers expect the sample, the descriptives, a test and an effect size with its interval per hypothesis, and captions that stand alone (Appelbaum et al., 2018). Every statistic here is simulated; the form is the lesson.

The shape of a within subjects results section

Open with the sample: 29 respondents (14 in Group A, 15 in Group B) after one exclusion. State once that every difference is Paint minus Balls. Take the hypotheses in order, each with the means and SDs, the mean difference with its interval, the paired t test, dz with its interval, and the Wilcoxon check in a clause. Put the order check after H1, which it qualifies. Report every planned test, including those that did not reach significance, and label exploratory results.

APA numbers for paired tests

APA style keeps numbers readable (simulated values): M = 28.49, SD = 7.75 with the unit once; the statistic with its degrees of freedom, t(28) = 5.27, then p, then the effect size with its interval, dz = 0.98, 95% CI [0.53, 1.42]. Give exact p values to three decimals (p = .016), and p < .001 only below that. Drop the leading zero for statistics that cannot exceed 1 (r = .48, ICC = .574). Match the sign to the direction stated in words, and write "did not reach significance," never "trend toward significance."

Discussion and publication: when attention and liking disagree, what limits the claim, and where the work goes

The discussion answers the client's question from section 01, says what the answer means for theory and practice, lists the limitations that bound it, and places the work in a literature. It is also where overclaiming happens, so every claim below is tied to a result or a source. The numbers are simulated.

What the result means

The central finding is a dissociation: Paint held the eyes on the product, while Balls was liked more and preferred by 22 of 29 viewers (simulated). Dwell time shows where attention went, not how the viewer felt about it. Emotional responses shape how viewers engage with video advertising (Teixeira et al., 2012), and physiological and self report measures capture different aspects of an advertisement (Venkatraman et al., 2015), so reading a long dwell as liking is the reverse inference consumer neuroscience warns against (Plassmann et al., 2015). Branding is a question of timing: brand presence over the course of a commercial affects whether viewers keep watching (Teixeira et al., 2010), and brand information placed where attention concentrates survives fast forwarding (Brasel & Gips, 2008); here the logo timing difference stayed uncertain. The hero elements, the balls and the paint, held about half of their active time, the kind of attention that visual complexity and stopping power research studies (Pieters et al., 2010). Fixations on the brand predict memory for it (Wedel & Pieters, 2000), which fits the recall correlation, rpb = .42, though it rests on five viewers who did not recall the brand.

Model discussion paragraph (simulated), quoted from section (c) of results_text.md

In these simulated data, the two commercials separated attention from attitude. Paint held the viewers' eyes on the television set for a larger share of the time the set was on screen (about seven percentage points, a large paired effect), yet Balls was the better liked and the more often preferred commercial, and the respondents who gave Paint more product attention were no more likely to like it. Dwell time shows where attention went, not how the viewer felt about what was seen, so the eye tracking result needs a criterion measure such as recall, stated intent, or choice before it is read as an advantage for the brand. Brand recall was higher among respondents who looked longer at the logo, but the association is correlational and rests on only five respondents who did not recall the brand. The order effect of about 3.6 percentage points shows why a within subjects test of commercials must be counterbalanced: because about half of the viewers saw each commercial first, the carryover averaged out of the commercial comparison, whereas a fixed order would have added it to the commercial seen second and overstated its advantage.

Limitations of a screen based test of commercials

  • Dynamic AOI decisions. Every metric depends on the active intervals, key frames, size, and padding the analyst drew. A short active interval gives a noisier share, and a more generous AOI catches more fixations. Report the AOI definitions with the results.
  • 60 Hz and a small logo. One sample every 16.67 ms makes TTFF and short fixations coarse, and a small AOI is sensitive to calibration accuracy; the simulated exclusion of R23 shows how tracking quality limits the sample.
  • Missing TTFF. Complete pairs, a 5,000 ms coding, and TTFF max. answer different questions and gave results of different precision. Say which you used.
  • Two stimuli. Story, music, product shot length, and end card layout all differ, so the result cannot be generalized to other commercials without a sample of them (Judd et al., 2012).
  • Order. Counterbalancing removed the order effect from the comparison but not from the noise.
  • The laboratory. Viewers watch without distraction and know they are observed, a demand effect that may inflate attention to the product and logo, and the rating slide may draw attention to what was just shown.
  • The sample. Twenty-nine simulated viewers, three hypotheses without correction, and an H2 result on either side of .05.

What other sensors would add

Eye tracking says where and when. Facial expression analysis, the subject of Workshop 3, records expressions moment by moment and can show whether the product shots in Paint or the hero moments in Balls coincide with smiles. Skin conductance, the subject of Workshop 4, records physiological arousal over the same timeline. Neither says why a viewer looked or replaces the liking question, and both bring their own processing steps and limits.

Replication, preregistration, and ethics

A preregistration time stamps the hypotheses, the primary metric for each, the AOI master list with active intervals, the exclusion rule, the TTFF coding, and the tests before the first viewer. A replication would add commercials as a stimulus sample and size the study for it (Brysbaert & Stevens, 2018). Gaze data can reveal more about a person than the person expects (Kröger et al., 2020), and the industry code sets out consent, privacy, and participant rights (Neuromarketing Science & Business Association, n.d.). At Jacksonville State a study that will be published or presented first goes to the IRB, and every session needs informed consent that names the gaze recording and a retention period.

Where this work is published, and what reviewers ask

  • Journal of Advertising, Journal of Advertising Research, and International Journal of Advertising, for advertising effects and executions.
  • Journal of Marketing Research and Journal of Consumer Research, for a theoretical contribution, for example on attention and attitude.
  • Behavior Research Methods, for a method contribution, where several eye tracking methods papers cited here appeared (Nyström et al., 2013) (Niehorster et al., 2018).

Reviewers ask whether the hypotheses and analysis were set in advance, whether the report meets the reporting standards (Appelbaum et al., 2018), whether the metrics are defined in the field's terms (Holmqvist et al., 2011), whether data quality is reported (Holmqvist et al., 2012), and whether the eye tracker showed something a survey could not.
